Almost everything written about locked doors in Korea assumes you are standing outside
one. The pinhole in the handle, the card trick, the locksmith at the door — all of it
is written for someone in a corridor.

This is the other one. **You are inside, the handle is turning and doing nothing, and
the emergency release is on the wrong side of the door.**

It happened to one of us last weekend, in one of our own houses. Here is the honest
version of it, because it is more useful than a tidy one.

He had his phone. He had signal. His wife was outside the building. There were windows
he could have opened and called down from. He was never, at any point, in the
slightest danger, and it took thirty minutes to get out.

**He panicked anyway.**

He was not frozen, either. He tried things — the postcard trick from a childhood
memory, a mental inventory of what in the room was heavy enough to break a door with.
Both of those were sensible. He was still rattled the entire time, and it still took
thirty minutes.

That is the part worth writing down: **being rattled and being capable are not
opposites, and you will be both.** He makes
content for a living and did not take a single photograph of any of it, which is why
there are none in this article. If you want a measure of what a stuck door does to
somebody, that is a better one than a picture would have been.

So the first thing this article does is the thing nobody did for him.

## First, the only question that matters

**Is anyone in danger?**

If there is fire or smoke, if you can smell gas, if someone in the room is hurt or
cannot answer you, or if there is a child or a baby involved:

**Call 119. Now. Let them open the door however they need to.**

They will break it if they have to, and that is the correct outcome. A door is a few
hundred thousand won. Do not sit behind one doing arithmetic. If you cannot speak,
**119 takes text messages**, and both 119 and 112 have interpreters — the details are
in [Korea's emergency numbers](/blog/korea-emergency-numbers-112-119/).

Everything below this line is for the other situation: you are fine, nothing is on
fire, and you simply cannot get out of a room.

## What 119 actually does when you text them

Here is the real exchange, because it settles three things people wonder about and one
they never think to ask.

![A text conversation with 119: a message reading "I am shut in a room, is there a way out?", an automatic reply saying 119 has queried the phone's location, a second saying a voice call would be faster, and two messages giving a national locksmith call centre number](./119-text-locksmith-referral.webp)

*119, Saturday 29 August. The message went out at 1:42pm. Between it and the 1:43
reply, a person from 119 rang back. We have removed the locksmith's last four digits —
it is not a company we know anything about, and we do not pass on referrals we have not
checked.*

**Texting 119 works.** It is the channel for when you cannot speak — or, as here, when
you can speak perfectly well and are too rattled to want to.

**They find you without being told where you are.** *119에서 긴급구조를 위해 귀하의
휴대전화 위치를 조회하였습니다* — *119 has queried your mobile phone's location for
emergency rescue.* That lands before a human has answered anything, and it means the
worst case is already covered: if you stop replying, they know where to come. If you
are stuck somewhere you could not describe in Korean anyway, that by itself is a reason
to send the message.

**But they would rather you rang.** The next reply says so outright — *all systems are
currently taking reports; reporting by voice call allows faster intake.* Text is the
fallback, not the front door. If you can talk, talk.

**And then the phone rang.** This is the part the screenshot cannot show, and it is the
part that matters most: **a person from 119 called back**, within a minute of the
message.

On that call he told them he was **standing the request down.** Nobody made that
decision for him and nobody argued him out of it. He was safe, and he said so.

**And they helped anyway.** Having just been told they were not needed, they gave him
the number of a national locksmith call centre over the phone — and then sent it by
text, twice, at 1:43 and 1:44, so he had it in writing rather than scribbled on whatever
was to hand.

That is the opposite of what people expect from an emergency number they have decided
not to bother. **119 did not brush him off. He stood it down, and they made sure he was
left with somewhere to go.**

So the useful thing to know is not that 119 will turn you away. It is this: **text them
and they will ring you, so keep the phone in your hand.** What happens next is a
conversation with a person, and you are part of it.

Had it been a real emergency they would have come, and they would have opened the door
however they needed to — which for a hollow wooden leaf in a wooden frame means breaking
it, and replacing it afterwards. A new door, a new frame if the frame goes with it, and
a carpenter.

So the money is real, and the decision about it is yours.

Here is the trap in it, from the person it happened to. He had the fire service on the
phone and told them to stand down — **not because he had weighed the risk and found it
low, but because he did not want to pay for his own door.** He was safe, and standing it
down happened to be the right call. That is not the reasoning he used. The reasoning he
used was that doors are expensive.

Everybody does that arithmetic. You will do it too. The problem is that it is the exact
same sentence in a person's head whether there is smoke under the door or not, and it
is a fine reason to wait when you are safe and a lethal one when you are not.

So decide the safety question first, on its own, before the money question exists. If
the answer is that someone is in danger, **the door is not part of the decision.**

## You almost certainly have more than you think

Panic narrows what you can see, and the first casualty is the list of things already in
your pocket. Before you touch the handle again, name them out loud:

- **Your phone, and whether it has signal.** Most of these end here.
- **Who is within earshot.** In the building, in the flat, on the other side of the
  wall. Somebody thirty seconds away beats a locksmith forty minutes away.
- **A window.** Not to climb out of — to call down from. A shout to the street is a
  perfectly good way to reach a person, and in a Korean neighbourhood at almost any
  hour there is somebody in it.
- **Time.** Nothing about a stuck door gets worse while you think. That is what makes
  it different from every other emergency, and it is the single most calming fact
  available to you.
- **The room itself, as a last resort.** A chair, a desk, the steel rail of a clothes
  hanger, a bed frame. **An interior door is a hollow leaf and a knob — it is not a
  vault**, and a knob loses to a chair leg. You should not do this over an
  inconvenience, and you would be paying for it. But knowing it is there is what stops
  the word *trapped* from being true, and it is worth knowing before you need it. In a
  real emergency, with 119 too far away, that is a door and you are a person.

Then work the list in that order. The reason the half-hour above was a half-hour and
not five minutes is that none of it got done first.

## What to try from the inside

Interior doors in Korea fail in a small number of ways, and two of them you can do
something about.

**Work out whether the latch is moving.** Turn the handle slowly and watch the edge of
the door. If the latch retracts and the door still will not come, the door is jammed
in the frame rather than locked — see the next section. If the handle turns limply and
the latch does not move at all, the mechanism has failed and this is not something you
will solve with technique.

**Push and pull while you turn.** A latch under sideways pressure from a swollen or
dropped frame binds. Take the weight of the door off it — push the door firmly toward
the frame, or pull it toward you — and turn the handle at the same time. This alone
opens a surprising number of doors that feel locked. It is the same fault that makes a
[keypad lock take your code and do nothing](/blog/door-lock-beeping-korea/).

**If the handle has come off on your side**, the square metal spindle it sits on is
still in the door. Anything that grips it and turns it will work — pliers, a coin in
the slot, a key shank. This is the one genuine self-rescue, and it depends entirely on
the spindle still being there.

## The two tricks you have heard of, and why neither works from in here

Everyone reaches for these first. Both are real, both are covered elsewhere on this
site, and **both are written for somebody standing in the corridor.**

**The pinhole is on the wrong side.** On a Korean bedroom or bathroom door the
emergency release is drilled into the outside face, because it exists so a parent can
open a door a child has locked from within. From your side it is not there at all.
Knowing that saves you ten minutes of running your fingers over a handle.

**The card will almost certainly not work either** — and this is the one that stings,
because most people have a memory of opening a door with a plastic sheet as a child and
assume the skill transfers.

Here is why it usually does not. A spring latch has one flat face and one sloped one.
The sloped face points the way the door travels as it shuts, so a card slid into the
gap can ride up it and push the latch back. **That slope is only reachable from the
side the door opens away from.**

So look at which way your door swings. **If you have to pull it toward you to open it,
the slope is facing the corridor, and there is no card, postcard or plastic sheet that
reaches it from where you are standing.** Korean bedroom doors usually open into the
room, which puts almost everyone who is stuck inside one on the wrong side of that
slope. One of us spent part of his thirty minutes proving it with a postcard.

And if the handle itself has failed — the thing that put you in there — then the latch
is not spring-loaded any more, it is jammed. At that point the slope does not matter,
because nothing is going to push a seized latch anywhere.

The card is still worth knowing. It works, well, from the other side:
[the card trick and when it works](/blog/locked-out-in-seoul-real-callout/).

## Two things not to do

**Do not climb out of the window — use it to shout.** A Korean walk-up has no fire
escape and no ledge worth the name, and the third floor of a three-storey house is a
fall that kills people. Climbing out is genuinely the most dangerous idea available to
you in that room, and it is the one that occurs to everybody at about the twenty-minute
mark. Opening the same window and calling down to the street is one of the best, and
it is free.

**Do not attack the handle.** Forcing a stuck mechanism turns it into a broken one, and
a broken one is harder for the person who arrives to open. If it is not moving, leave
it and spend the effort on reaching somebody.

## Reaching someone when you are the one stuck

You will get out faster through a person than through the door.

- **Anyone in the building.** A neighbour with a screwdriver beats a locksmith with a
  van, because the neighbour is already there. This is how ours ended — thirty minutes,
  no damage, no invoice.
- **The building office (관리실)** if there is one, or the landlord.
- **Us, or whoever manages your building**, with a photo of the handle. Half of these
  turn out to be the push-and-pull above, which costs nothing and takes ten seconds to
  explain.

Say in the first message **that you are on the inside.** It changes everything about
what gets recommended and how quickly, and it is the detail people leave out.

## When you are the one outside the door

The mirror image happens more often than the real thing, and it is worth knowing how
to check.

A resident of one of our houses called us because the bathroom door was locked and she
could hear water running behind it at an hour when nobody was usually up. That is a
reasonable thing to be alarmed by. It was somebody having a shower.

**Knock and say your name before you assume the worst.** If there is no answer and
something sounds wrong — a fall, a tap that has been running far too long, no response
from someone you know is in there — then it stops being a privacy question and
[119 is the right call](/blog/korea-emergency-numbers-112-119/), before any locksmith.

## What it costs, if it comes to a locksmith

A stuck interior door is at the cheap end of locksmith work when it opens without
damage, and expensive the moment it does not. The
[guide ranges and the running log of real jobs](/locksmith) are on our locksmith page.
What moves the number is the same as ever: whether it opens without damage, the hour,
and how far someone has to travel.

If the handle has to be replaced, the part is not the expensive bit. A standard interior
handle set is a few tens of thousands of won at a hardware shop or online.

## The thing we got wrong

We replace every door handle when we open a house. It is cheap, it is quick, and it is
the sort of maintenance nobody notices.

In this house, one handle looked perfectly fine, so we left it.

**That is the one that trapped somebody.** A handle that looks fine tells you nothing
about the spring inside it, and the replacement costs less than a single callout. We
have gone back to no exceptions, and the reason is written above.

If you rent: **a handle that sticks, feels loose, or turns further than it should is a
repair request, not a quirk.** Send it to your landlord in writing now, while it is
still an inconvenience. It is much harder to have that conversation from the inside.

## The Korean part

Being stuck in a room in a country where you do not speak the language is worse than
the door deserves. You cannot explain the handle, you cannot describe the building,
and the number you were given rings out in Korean.

If anyone is in danger, **119**, and let them take the door.
