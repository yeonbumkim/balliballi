You're standing outside your door in Seoul, pressing the keypad, and nothing is
lighting up. Before you call anyone or start googling locksmiths, try the one
trick that gets most people back inside in ten minutes — it costs about ₩2,000
at the convenience store downstairs.

## Try this first

### Check that it's actually the battery

A dead battery looks like this: you touch the keypad and **no lights come on at
all**, or the numbers flash for a second and immediately go dark. If the keypad
lights up normally and beeps but the door still won't open, skip ahead — that's
a different problem, and the battery trick won't help.

### The 9V battery trick

Almost every Korean digital lock (Samsung, Yale, Gateman, Hugel and the rest)
has a hidden backup feature for exactly this moment: **two small metal contacts
on the outside of the lock**, usually on the bottom edge or under a little
cover, often marked "9V".

1. Go to the nearest convenience store and buy a **9V square battery** — the
   chunky rectangular one with both terminals on top. While you're there, grab
   a pack of AA or AAA batteries too; you'll need them once you're inside.
2. Find the two round or oblong contacts on the lock's outer body.
3. Press the 9V battery against them and **hold it there for 10–20 seconds**.
   The keypad will wake up.
4. Keep the battery pressed against the contacts and enter your door code as
   usual.

Once you're in, slide open the battery cover on the inside unit — no
screwdriver needed on most models — and replace all the internal batteries,
not just some of them. Then leave the 9V battery somewhere you can reach
without entering the apartment, like your mailbox. Future you will be grateful.

### Ask the building for help

If the trick doesn't work, or you can't find the contacts, two people may be
able to open your door without any tools: your **landlord** and, in most
apartment and officetel buildings, the **security office**. Many buildings keep
a master registration for their locks. It's free, and it's the fastest option
during daytime hours.

## When to stop and call someone

> **🚨 Real emergency? Call 119 first — not us, not a locksmith.**

> fire service opens doors **free of charge** in genuine emergencies and
> gets there faster than any locksmith ever will — and interpretation is
> available on the line. This is *only* for true emergencies; for
> everything else, 119 is the wrong number and the options below are
> yours. (Not sure which number does what? We laid out
> [112, 119 and the rest](/blog/korea-emergency-numbers-112-119/),
> including the fact that you can text them.)

Stop poking at the lock and get a professional involved if any of these is
true:

- The keypad **lights up and beeps normally but the door won't open** — that's
  usually a worn motor or clutch inside the lock body, not a power problem.
- You **never knew the code** (new move-in, sublet, or a code someone else set).
- There's a **physical key snapped off** inside an old-style cylinder.
- You've entered the wrong code repeatedly and the lock is now in **tamper
  alarm mode**, flashing and refusing input.

Forcing a digital lock yourself tends to turn a ₩50,000 opening into a full
lock replacement. A good locksmith opens non-destructively first, and drills
only as a last resort — if someone proposes drilling as the first step, ask
why before agreeing.

## The Korean part

This is where most non-Korean speakers get stuck, even after doing everything
above correctly.

- **The phone call.** Neighborhood locksmith shops (열쇠집, *yeolsoe-jip*)
  rarely speak English, and most jobs are arranged by phone in Korean —
  explaining your situation, your lock brand, and negotiating a time.
- **The address.** Korean addresses are given in a different order than Western
  ones, and getting one digit of the 동/호 (building/unit number) wrong sends
  the locksmith to the wrong door.
- **The verification.** Explaining in Korean that your proof of residency is an
  English-language lease or a foreign ID adds another layer of confusion.
- **Airbnbs.** For short-term stays, the locksmith needs authorization from
  your host — which means someone has to reach the host, in Korean, and relay
  the situation accurately.
