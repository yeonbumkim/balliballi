At some point living here, Korean stops being a hobby and starts
being paperwork. A university application asks for a level. A visa
points table gives you credit for one. An employer asks which one
you have.

The thing they are all asking about is **TOPIK**, and almost
nobody explains it to you before you need it.

## What TOPIK actually is

**TOPIK — the Test of Proficiency in Korean** — is the official
Korean-language exam, run by a Korean government institute and held
several times a year, both inside Korea and at test centres
overseas.

Two things surprise people:

**You do not pick your level.** You pick a *paper*, sit it, and
your level is decided by your score. There is no "I'll take the
level 4 exam."

**There are two papers:**

| Paper | Awards | Sections |
| ----- | ------ | -------- |
| **TOPIK I** | Level 1–2 | Listening, reading |
| **TOPIK II** | Level 3–6 | Listening, writing, reading |

TOPIK I is for beginners and has no writing section. TOPIK II is
the one that matters for most official purposes, and it includes
written composition — which is where unprepared candidates lose
most of their marks.

The writing paper is **four questions**, and the shape of it
surprises people: two short answers where you supply the missing
phrase yourself, then two essays. One essay is an everyday topic.
The other asks for your opinion on an issue and expects it argued.
Seventy minutes, no multiple choice, nothing to guess at.

The test runs **six times a year in Korea** — three of those
sittings are Korea-only, three are held overseas as well.

## Your score picks the level — here are the numbers

This is the part that is hard to find in one place, and it is worth
knowing before you book anything, because it tells you what you are
actually aiming at.

| Paper | Total | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 | Level 5 | Level 6 |
| ----- | ----- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| **TOPIK I** | 200 | **80** | **140** | — | — | — | — |
| **TOPIK II** | 300 | — | — | **120** | **150** | **190** | **230** |

Read it as a floor: score 150 on TOPIK II and you are level 4;
score 149 and you are level 3. There is no rounding and no appeal to
how close you were.

Two things fall out of that table. **The jump from 3 to 4 is 30
points and the jump from 4 to 5 is 40** — the middle of the scale is
where the work is. And **sitting the wrong paper caps you**: TOPIK I
cannot award you level 3 no matter how well you do, so if level 3 or
above is what you need, TOPIK II is the paper even if it feels a
stretch.

## What the levels are actually for

This is the part worth knowing *before* you choose a study plan,
because the level you need depends entirely on why you are taking
it:

- **University admission.** Korean-taught undergraduate and graduate
  programmes generally want a mid-to-upper TOPIK II level. Each
  university sets its own bar, and they are not identical
- **Visas and residency.** Korea's points-based residency tracks
  award credit for Korean ability, and TOPIK is the standard proof
- **Employment.** Korean companies hiring foreign staff often state
  a level in the job posting
- **Scholarships**, including the government ones, set their own
  minimums

The practical implication: **find out the exact number your target
asks for, then aim one level above it.** Nobody has ever regretted
that; plenty of people have missed a cutoff by a few points.

Because requirements and test dates change, check the official
site — **topik.go.kr** — rather than a blog post, including this
one, for anything you are going to plan around.

## How people actually study

There is no shortage of apps and textbooks. What separates people
who get the level from people who keep almost getting it is duller
than that.

**Past papers, early and often.** TOPIK is a specific exam with a
specific shape, and the single highest-return activity is sitting
old papers under time pressure. Past questions are published. Doing
one cold, right at the start, tells you your real starting point
better than any placement quiz.

**The clock is the real opponent** — particularly in reading. Many
candidates know enough Korean and still run out of time, because
they read every passage the way they read a book. Learning to skim
for the answer is a test skill, not a language skill, and it is
trainable.

**Writing is where TOPIK II is won or lost.** It is the section
self-studiers neglect, because it is the only one you cannot mark
yourself. Composition needs a human to correct it — and this is the
single strongest argument for having a teacher rather than an app.

**Vocabulary by level, not by vibe.** Level-graded vocabulary lists
exist for a reason. Learning words you like is pleasant; learning
the words the exam uses is what moves the score.

**Listening rewards volume.** Drama with subtitles is entertainment.
Drama without subtitles, radio, podcasts — that is training.

## Self-study, class, or a teacher?

Honestly, all three work, for different people:

- **Self-study** is cheapest and works if you are disciplined and
  already have a grammar base. Its weak point is speaking and
  writing — the two things nobody can correct alone
- **Language institutes** attached to universities are thorough and
  structured, and they are a serious time commitment on a fixed
  timetable
- **A tutor** is the flexible middle: your schedule, your weak
  areas, and someone marking the writing you cannot mark yourself

If you are working, the tutor route usually survives contact with
real life better than a fixed class timetable.

## Studying here, living here

One thing worth saying to anyone about to start: **the fastest
progress comes from needing the language**, not from studying more
hours. People who handle their own deliveries, appointments and
building-office conversations in Korean improve faster than people
who study twice as hard and outsource every real interaction.
