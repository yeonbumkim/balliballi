You tapped in at Hongdae and out at Gangnam and the gate took ₩1,550. Your
friend rode two stops and paid the same. Someone else went to Suwon and paid
more. The Seoul subway is not a flat fare and it is not a zone system — it
charges by **how far you actually travelled**, and it only knows how far you
went if you tap on the way out.

Here is the whole fare structure, taken from
[Seoul Metro's official fare page](http://www.seoulmetro.co.kr/en/page.do?menuIdx=348),
turned into the numbers you'll actually meet. *Fares below are as of
2026-08.*

## The base fare, and what pushes it up

Everything starts from one number and grows by distance. The shortest route
between your two stations is what counts — not the route you actually rode,
so taking a scenic detour doesn't cost you extra.

| Rider | Base fare (up to 10 km) | 10–50 km | Past 50 km |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adult** | **₩1,550** | +₩100 per 5 km | +₩100 per 8 km |
| **Youth** (13–19) | ₩900 | +₩80 per 5 km | +₩80 per 8 km |
| **Child** (6–12) | ₩550 | +₩50 per 5 km | +₩50 per 8 km |

These numbers are newer than much of what's written about Seoul in English.
The metropolitan fare rose from **₩1,400 to ₩1,550** for an adult card on
**28 June 2025**, across Seoul, Gyeonggi and Incheon together. If a guide
quotes you ₩1,400 — and plenty still do — it was written before that date.
The distance surcharges above were left unchanged in the same revision.

Those are **transportation card** fares — a T-money or Cashbee card, or a
card-linked phone. If you don't have one yet, our
[T-money card guide](/blog/t-money-card-korea-guide/) covers buying one at a
convenience store in about ninety seconds.

Worked example, adult card: a 25 km ride is 10 km of base fare plus three
more 5 km bands, so **₩1,550 + ₩300 = ₩1,850**. Most rides inside Seoul
proper never leave the base fare at all — the city is smaller than the map
makes it look.

Two things stretch the distance beyond the ordinary rules:

- **Past 50 km**, the surcharge interval widens from every 5 km to every
  8 km. Long trips get proportionally cheaper, not more expensive.
- **Outside the Greater Seoul area** — the Pyeongtaek–Sinchang and
  Gapyeong–Chuncheon stretches — the metropolitan fare is applied first, then
  ₩100 is added for **every 4 km** of the outer section. That's the one part
  of the system that gets *more* expensive per kilometre, and it's why a run
  out to Chuncheon costs what it does.

You never have to do this arithmetic yourself. Naver Map and Kakao Map both
show the exact fare for a route before you ride it, and they're the same apps
you're already using for directions.

## Tap out. Every time.

This is the single rule that costs travellers real money, and it's the one
nobody tells you at the airport.

Korean fare gates read your card **on the way in and on the way out**. The
tap-in starts the journey; the tap-out is what tells the system how far you
went. Skip the tap-out — walk through an open gate behind someone, or use an
exit where you didn't touch the reader — and two things happen:

1. **You lose your free transfer.** Each leg of the trip gets billed as a
   separate journey at full base fare.
2. **On distance-based routes you can be charged the maximum fare** for that
   line, because the system has no idea where you got off and assumes the
   worst.

The reader is the panel that lights up and beeps. If it didn't beep, it
didn't read. This applies to buses too, which is where most people slip up —
tapping on when boarding feels natural, tapping off when alighting doesn't.

## Transfers are where the system pays you back

Seoul doesn't charge you per vehicle. It charges you per **journey**, across
subway and bus together, and that's the part that makes the fares above
better than they look.

- Subway → bus? One journey.
- Bus → subway? One journey.
- Bus → bus → subway? Still one journey.

You pay the base fare once and keep going, up to four transfers — five legs
in total. Beyond 10 km the usual ₩100-per-5 km surcharge applies to the
combined distance, so crossing the whole city on three vehicles costs a card
user a fraction of what it costs someone paying each leg separately. Where
several types of transport are involved, the **highest base fare among them**
is the one charged.

Two conditions:

- **The window is 30 minutes** from tapping off one vehicle to tapping onto
  the next. Miss it and the next leg is a fresh journey at full fare.
- **Between 21:00 and 07:00 the window doubles to 60 minutes.** Late-night
  connections are thinner, and the fare system quietly accounts for it. This
  is worth knowing if you're heading home after dinner and the bus you want
  is twenty-five minutes out — you're still inside the window.

For the metropolitan, Gyeonggi express and Incheon express buses, the free
transfer distance runs to 30 km rather than 10 km before surcharges start.

## The 6:30am discount nobody mentions

From the start of service until **6:30am**, transportation card users get
**20% off** the fare. Not a special ticket, not a registration — you tap as
normal and the gate charges less.

The one exclusion: it doesn't apply to a leg you're transferring onto after
already riding something else. It's a discount for starting your journey
early, not for being mid-journey at 6:29.

Most visitors will never use this. But if you're catching an early train out
of Seoul Station, or you're on the way to a fish market that opens before
dawn, you're already awake and inside the window — and the discount applies
whether or not you knew about it.

## Single-use tickets cost more — and teenagers pay full price

If you're not using a card, you buy a single-journey ticket from a machine in
the station — the screen at the top of this page, with English on it. It
costs **₩100 more** than the card fare, so an adult base fare becomes
**₩1,650**.

The machine will also take a **₩500 deposit** on top. That isn't a fare —
it's a returnable deposit on the plastic card itself, and you get it back by
feeding the used ticket into a deposit refund machine, which sits inside
every station. So an adult single ride actually costs ₩2,150 at the machine
and ₩1,650 once you've collected the ₩500. Miss the refund machine on the way
out and you've simply paid ₩500 for a piece of plastic.

Here's the trap for families: **on single-journey tickets, youths pay the
adult fare.** The ₩900 youth rate exists only on transportation cards. A
child (6–12) still pays ₩550 either way. This isn't an oversight in the
tables — the June 2025 revision raised the youth single-journey price in step
with the adult one, keeping the two identical.

| | Card | Single-journey ticket |
|---|---|---|
| Adult | ₩1,550 | ₩1,650 |
| Youth (13–19) | ₩900 | **₩1,650 — adult price** |
| Child (6–12) | ₩550 | ₩550 |

For a family of four with two teenagers, a week of single-journey tickets
versus cards is a meaningful gap — and single-journey tickets earn **no
transfer credit** either. Buy the cards.

There's one more restriction the fare tables don't mention, and it's printed
on the ticket machine screen itself — you can read it on the cover photo
above: **at Seoul Station, a single-journey
ticket or a commuter ticket can't be used to transfer between Line 1, Line 4
and the Airport Railroad on one side and the Gyeongui–Jungang Line on the
other.** Those platforms sit behind separate gates. A transportation card
handles the move; a paper ticket doesn't. Seoul Station is exactly where a
visitor is most likely to attempt that transfer, coming in from the airport.

## Who rides free

The system carries a number of riders at no charge:

- **Seniors aged 65 and over**
- **Registered disabled persons**, including one guardian for severely
  disabled riders
- **Persons of national merit**, including one guardian in the applicable
  categories
- **Toddlers under 6** — one guardian may bring up to three free. From the
  fourth toddler, the child fare applies.

The senior and disability exemptions are tied to Korean registration, so a
visiting 70-year-old on a tourist visa pays the adult fare. The toddler
exemption has no such condition — young children ride free regardless of
where you're from.

## Staying longer: commuter tickets

If you're commuting daily rather than sightseeing, the **정기권
(jeonggigwon)** — commuter ticket — is priced as your base fare × 44 rides,
less 15%. For travel inside Seoul that works out to **₩68,200**, and
distance-based versions run up to ₩129,000 across 18 pricing tiers.

It gives you **60 rides within 30 days**, and it expires at whichever comes
first — the 60th ride or the 30th day. Since 44 rides' worth of money buys
60 rides, it pays off from roughly the 45th ride onward. That's a genuine
commute, not a holiday.

Seoul also runs short-term tourist passes and a monthly unlimited pass, but
**that side of the system is being restructured through 2026** as the city's
Climate Card is wound down and its benefits move to the national K-Pass. We
track what the ticket machines are actually saying about it in the
[T-money guide](/blog/t-money-card-korea-guide/#monthly-passes-dont-build-a-plan-around-them-right-now),
because it has been changing faster than most guides update. The plain fares
in this article are the part that isn't changing.

## The Korean part

The fares themselves are handled in English: ticket machines have an English
mode, the gates are pictographic, and Naver Map will tell you what a ride
costs before you take it.

Where English runs out is everything *around* the fare. A card that stops
reading, a gate that charges you the maximum because a tap-out didn't
register, a refund on an unused single-journey ticket, a lost card with money
on it — those are conversations at the station office, in Korean, with a
staff member who may not have English.
