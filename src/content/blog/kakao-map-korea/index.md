If you have already installed [Naver Map](/blog/naver-map-korea/), you have solved
getting from A to B. Kakao Map solves a different problem: **deciding whether B is
worth going to.**

That is not a small distinction in a country where the difference between a very good
restaurant and a forgettable one is often two doors apart on the same alley, and both
look identical from outside.

**Kakao Map** — 카카오맵:

- **iOS:** [Kakao Map on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/kr/app/id304608425)
- **Android:** [Kakao Map on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=net.daum.android.map)

English works about as well as it does in Naver Map, so this is not a Korean-only
tool. The rest of this is what to do with it.

## The four-star rule

Here is the local habit, stated plainly: **when you are choosing where to eat, the
rating that means something is the one on Kakao Map.**

A restaurant sitting **above four stars there is very unlikely to disappoint you.**
Not "might be good" — actively hard to regret. Koreans use it this way, and it is
the single most useful thing a visitor can borrow from how people here actually
behave.

Below four is not a verdict of bad. It is a signal to read the written reviews rather
than trust the number, which is a different amount of work.

**This is not a partnership.** Nobody pays us to say it and we take no commission from
anyone — it is what you end up believing after living here and eating out for years.
The reasons it holds are in the next section rather than in a contract, and you can
check them yourself.

## Why these ratings hold up

Two reasons, and neither is that Kakao is a better company.

**They have history.** Kakao Map's ratings have been accumulating continuously.
Naver Map's star ratings only came back in **April 2026, after five years away** —
they were removed in 2021 because people were using them to punish businesses for no
reason. They have not had time to settle, and Naver has deliberately positioned them
as a secondary indicator alongside its keyword reviews rather than as the headline
number.

**They sit in a quieter place.** Naver's place pages are the front door to a very
large ecosystem of blogs and sponsored posts. That is genuinely useful when you want
depth — photographs of every dish, a write-up of the neighbourhood. It is less useful
when what you want is a number you can trust in thirty seconds while standing on a
corner deciding.

So the working split is: **find it in Google, route to it in Naver, judge it on
Kakao.**

## Send the place into a KakaoTalk chat

This is the feature nobody mentions and everybody here uses.

Plans in Korea are made in KakaoTalk. Not email, not a calendar invite — a group chat.
Kakao Map is built by the same company, so **sending a location into a chat is one
tap**, and it arrives as a proper map card rather than a pasted link that opens a
browser and asks you to log in.

For a foreign resident this matters more than it does for a local, because you are
the person in the chat least able to describe where you mean in Korean. Sending the
pin removes the sentence you could not have written.

It pairs with the other habit worth having: agree a **numbered subway exit** and send
the pin as well. Between the two, nobody is standing on the wrong side of a road.

## Roadview, including what a place used to look like

Kakao's street-level imagery is called **로드뷰 (Roadview)**, and it does something
the obvious version does not: **it keeps the older captures**, so you can slide back
and see the same spot in previous years.

Two uses that come up constantly:

- **Checking a place still exists.** Korean small businesses turn over fast. If the
  current capture shows a shutter and the one from two years ago shows the restaurant
  you are looking for, you have your answer before you walk twenty minutes.
- **Looking at a street before you commit to living on it.** What a building looks
  like, whether the alley is lit, how far the nearest convenience store really is.
  For anyone flat-hunting, ten minutes in Roadview is worth an hour of listings.

## Buses in real time

Both apps do live transit and both do it well. Kakao's bus information is worth
knowing about because it goes granular — position, arrival, and how full the thing
is — and it has been pushed further for big events, where hundreds of Seoul routes
have been given ultra-precise tracking for the day.

If you are waiting at a stop in the rain, the app that tells you whether the bus is
four minutes away or eleven is the app you keep.

## The seasonal maps

Kakao does things Naver does not bother with, and the best-known is the **cherry
blossom map** — where the blossom has actually opened, updated as the front moves
north, rather than a guess based on last year.

Korea's blossom season is about ten days in any given place and the whole country
plans around it. Being able to see where it is out today, instead of arriving to bare
branches, is the kind of thing that turns a trip.

Similar seasonal layers appear through the year. It is worth opening the app in spring
even if you navigate with something else.
