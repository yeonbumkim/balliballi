A Dane in Seoul described the problem better than any guide has. He had
two weeks left on an Airbnb, about **₩1,000,000 a month** to spend, and
wanted somewhere furnished for three to six months. He had been through
Zigbang and Dabang, and kept hitting the same two walls: the places that
would take a short lease **would not allow address registration**, or the
owner **would not rent to a non-Korean**.

He asked whether he was looking in the wrong places, or whether that was
simply the market.

It is simply the market. But the thread that followed is one of the most
useful things written about Korean housing, because thirty people
answered with what they had actually done — and no two of them did the
same thing.

## The three things you cannot have at once

Every route below is a trade between the same three variables. **You will
usually get two.**

| | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Short** | Three to six months, rather than the standard one- or two-year lease |
| **Cheap deposit** | Hundreds of thousands rather than ₩10,000,000+ |
| **Registrable** | You can register the address for your ARC |

Long lease and a big deposit buys you everything. A short, deposit-free
room usually costs you the registration. Somewhere in between is the
answer for most people, and which two you pick depends on one question:
**do you actually need the address?**

## When the address matters

Skip this if you are here for under 90 days on a tourist stamp.

If you hold or are applying for an **ARC**, you are required to report
where you live, and the address on the card is the one everything else
hangs off. The Dane in that thread named the trap most people meet later:
the **PCCC** — the personal customs clearance code you need to receive
parcels from abroad — is tied to your registered address.

So an unregistrable room is not merely untidy. It quietly blocks a
customs code, a delivery, and a bank's address check. **Decide this
before you look at rooms**, because it removes about half the market and
there is no point falling for a flat you cannot use.

## The routes people actually took

Straight from that thread and the one on r/seoul, with what each is good
for.

| Route | Deposit | Minimum stay | Address registration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Airbnb** | None | Nightly | ❌ |
| **33m2 app** | Around ₩330,000 | Short — extendable in three-month blocks | ❌ usually |
| **enkostay** | None | About 15 days to a month | ⚠️ Ask — one tenant confirmed it in writing first |
| **단기임대** via Naver Real Estate or Danggeun | About one month's rent | Flexible | ❌ mostly |
| **Coliving** (Mangrove, Localstitch and similar) | Low | A month or more | ✅ generally |
| **Share house** | Low | A month or more | ⚠️ Varies by house |
| **Premium goshiwon** | None | Monthly | ⚠️ Varies |
| **월세 through an agent (부동산)** | **₩10,000,000+** | One to two years | ✅ |

Two observations from the people who did it.

**The apps do the short end better than agents do.** Several tenants
independently landed on **33m2** and **enkostay** — one found 7 pyeong
near Sadang for ₩850,000 and cancelled an Airbnb that had been costing
₩1,300,000 for something smaller. The deposits are the striking part:
a few hundred thousand won, not ten million.

**Agents can do short leases, but you must move fast.** One resident
went through an English-speaking agent found via Zigbang and said the
catch is timing — **with short leases you have to be ready to move in
within one to four days.** That is a different kind of search from
browsing for a fortnight.

## What ₩1,000,000 actually gets you

The honest answers in that thread were blunter than the listings.

> *"For ₩1M and no deposit you're stuck with shitty landlords and/or
> goshiwons. It isn't enough for a studio in a good location in Seoul."*

Raise it to ₩1.5–2M and the market opens. Keep it at ₩1M and you are
choosing between a small room in a good area and a bigger one further
out — which is why the same thread kept recommending **Gyeonggi**, one
stop past the city line, where the same money buys noticeably more.

**A premium goshiwon is the other honest answer at that budget.** One
resident who gave up on rooms took one and did not regret it: new
interior, cleaned three times a week, rice and ramen included, shared
bathroom. Their advice on what to check is worth repeating — **concrete
walls for soundproofing, a window, and a fridge.**

## The listing prices are not the prices

Someone posted a tidy table of average rents by district — Gangnam
studios at ₩900,000, Hongdae at ₩720,000 — and the comments took it
apart
[on r/seoul](https://www.reddit.com/r/seoul/comments/1lmc0pd/whats_the_real_rent_in_seoul_in_2025_i_compared_6/).
Two objections matter, and both are things to carry into your own search.

**"This means nothing without deposit."** The top reply, and correct.
Korean rent trades against the deposit — put more down, pay less monthly
— so a monthly figure with no deposit attached describes nothing at all.
Residents answering with real contracts made the point concrete: a
6.5-pyeong flat in Gangnam at ₩20,000,000 deposit and ₩1,300,000 rent; a
5-pyeong officetel in Jongno at ₩20,000,000 and ₩1,100,000. **Always
quote both numbers, and always ask what the other one would be.** How to
read that trade is in
[what the deposit really tells you](/blog/what-to-check-before-renting-seoul/).

**"Those listings are fake."** The second objection, and the one nobody
enjoys hearing. Bait listings — attractive rooms posted to get you
through the door, then unavailable when you arrive — are common enough
that averages built from listings are averages of things you cannot rent.
The person who posted the table conceded it, and added the other half
himself: **landlords sometimes quote foreigners noticeably higher prices.**

Both are reasons to go and stand in the room, and to have someone who
reads Korean look at the listing before you travel to it.

## Where to look, in order

1. **Decide on the address.** Registrable or not. Everything follows.
2. **Decide your deposit ceiling.** Under a million, you are on apps and
   in coliving. Ten million opens the ordinary rental market.
3. **Search the Korean platforms** — Zigbang (직방), Dabang (다방),
   Peter Pan's Good Room (피터팬의 좋은 방 구하기) — and the short-stay
   apps alongside them.
4. **Get the listing checked before you travel to it**, and treat any
   unusually good price as unverified until someone has stood in it.
5. **Confirm registration in writing** before you pay anything. One
   tenant in that thread did exactly this and it is the single best habit
   in this article.

## The Korean part

Every platform above runs in Korean, and the verification wall sits in
front of most of them — Naver bookings and many app sign-ups want a
Korean phone number in your own name.

The heavier problem is the one the Dane hit: **owners who would rather
not rent to a foreigner.** It is not universal and it is not always
hostile — often it is a landlord who does not want to conduct a tenancy
in a language they do not speak, and who assumes a short stay means an
empty room in four months. A Korean-speaking intermediary changes that
conversation more than any amount of budget does.
