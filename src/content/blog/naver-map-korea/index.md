You land, you open Google Maps, you type your hotel, and it shows you where it is
and then refuses to tell you how to walk there.

This surprises almost everyone, and it is not a bug or a bad signal. **Korean law
has restricted the export of detailed domestic map data**, so Google has not held the
data it would need to route you on foot or by car inside Korea. Transit directions
mostly work. Walking and driving do not.

The fix is one download, and it is worth doing before you get on the plane rather
than while standing in an arrivals hall on airport wifi.

## This is changing — but not yet

In **February 2026 the Korean government approved Google's request to export
high-precision map data**, ending a dispute that had run since 2007 and had been
refused twice on national-security grounds tied to the unfinished war with the North.

The approval came with conditions rather than a switch being flipped: the data is to
be processed on servers inside Korea first, cleared by the government before it
leaves, and stripped or blurred where military and other sensitive sites are
concerned.

**So Google Maps will eventually route you here, and at the time of writing it still
does not.** If you are reading this on the plane, download Naver Map. If you are
reading it much later, check Google first — and then read the rest of this anyway,
because the reasons people here keep Naver are not only about turn-by-turn.

## Get it now

**Naver Map** — 네이버 지도 — is the one most people here use.

- **iOS:** [Naver Map on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/naver-maps-navigation/id311867728)
- **Android:** [Naver Map on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.nhn.android.nmap)

Kakao Map (카카오맵) is the other one, and it is also good. Most people end up with
both eventually. Start with Naver — as of early 2026 it had roughly 28 million
monthly users in Korea, against about 12 million for Kakao Map and 9 million for
Google Maps. It is simply the one the people around you are using.

## Set the language first

There is a good chance it is already done. **If your phone's system language is not
Korean, the app follows it** — so many visitors open it and find it in English
without touching anything. If it opens in Korean anyway, the language is a setting
inside the app, and it is the first thing to change rather than the thing you
discover in week three.

**English is not the only option.** Naver Map also runs in Chinese and Japanese,
which is worth knowing if English is not your first language either.

## What comes through in your language

More than the interface, which is the part people assume and then underestimate.

**Public transport, including the live parts.** Arrival times, alternative routes
when the one you wanted is slow, and the boarding and alighting instructions — which
bus door, which subway car end, which transfer. Real-time arrivals are the whole
value of a transit app in Korea, and they are not held back for Korean speakers.

**The subway map itself in English**, with first and last train times as well as
live arrivals. The last-train time is the one that matters at half past midnight.

**Driving directions with English voice guidance.** Not just the place names on
screen — the voice too, so you can keep your eyes on the road.

### The one thing deliberately left in Korean

Turn-by-turn place names in car navigation **stay in Korean on purpose.** It reads
like an oversight and it is the opposite of one: the name the app says is the name
painted on the sign you are about to pass. Translate it and you would be comparing an
English word against a Korean gantry at 100km/h.

Once you know that is intentional, it becomes useful rather than confusing. Match the
shape of the characters, not the meaning.

## What else it does better

**Walking directions that work**, which is the whole reason you are installing it.

**Subway exits by number.** Large Korean stations have fifteen exits spread over half
a kilometre, and coming up the wrong one costs you ten minutes and a road crossing.
Naver names the exit, and the exits are numbered on the ground.

**Indoor floor plans** for large stations, malls and underground shopping streets,
which are their own cities down there.

**Street-level imagery**, useful for checking what a place looks like before you set
off to find a door in an alley.

## Where it will fight you

**Searching in English is unreliable for small places.** Chains and landmarks are
fine. A neighbourhood restaurant is often listed only under its Korean name, and the
romanisation you would guess is not the one in the database.

The way around it is to search the Korean name, and the way to get the Korean name is
to copy it from wherever you found the place — a blog, an Instagram post, a friend's
message. Paste, do not retype.

**Addresses come in two systems.** Korea has road-name addresses (도로명주소) and the
older lot-number ones (지번). Both are still in use, and a place will sometimes be
findable under one and not the other. If an address fails, try the other format
before deciding the place does not exist.

**Directions assume you can read the signs at the end.** The app will get you to the
right building; the sign on the door may still be in Korean only.

## Kakao Map, and why people keep both

Kakao Map is the same category of tool with different strengths. Some find its
walking directions clearer; its coverage of small businesses is not identical, so a
place missing from one is sometimes present in the other.

There is no need to choose on day one. Install Naver, and add Kakao the first time
Naver cannot find something — which will happen.

- **iOS:** [Kakao Map on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/kr/app/id304608425)
- **Android:** [Kakao Map on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=net.daum.android.map)

Between the two of them, a place that exists in Korea is findable.

### For restaurants, trust Kakao's ratings

This is the local habit visitors miss, and it is worth installing Kakao for on its
own. **When you are deciding where to eat, the rating that means something is the one
on Kakao Map.** A restaurant sitting above four there is very unlikely to disappoint
you.

Naver's are newer and thinner than they look. **Star ratings only returned to Naver
Map in April 2026, after five years away** — they were removed in 2021 because people
were using them to punish businesses for no reason — and they have come back
deliberately positioned as a secondary indicator alongside the keyword reviews
("친절해요", "가성비가 좋아요") rather than as the headline number. They have not had
time to accumulate.

There is also a difference in what surrounds them. Naver place pages sit inside a
much larger ecosystem of blogs and sponsored posts, which is excellent for depth and
less useful when what you want is a quick honest verdict on whether the noodles are
any good.

So the full habit is: **find it in Google, route to it in Naver, and check what Kakao
thinks of it before you commit to dinner.** Google Maps is the next section.

## What Google Maps is still for

Keep it. It is still the best thing for **finding** a place rather than routing to it
— searching in English, reading reviews written in English, and saving pins before
you arrive.

The workable habit is: **search in Google Maps, navigate in Naver Map.** Copy the
Korean name across when the English search fails.
