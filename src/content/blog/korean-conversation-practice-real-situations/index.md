You have done the app. You know more words than you can use. And
then someone at a counter says one sentence you did not expect, and
your whole vocabulary evaporates.

This is not a knowledge problem. It is a **rehearsal** problem, and
it is the reason people who "know" more Korean often speak less of
it than people who know less.

## Why textbook Korean does not survive contact

Three things go wrong between the book and the street.

**Real Korean is faster and shorter.** Textbook sentences are
complete. Actual counter Korean is clipped, dropped-particle,
half-sentence — and the polite endings you learned get contracted
into something that sounds nothing like the recording.

**You learned the question, not the answer.** Most courses teach
you to *ask*. What breaks people is the reply, which arrives at
full speed with a word you have never heard.

**You are practising the wrong situations.** Chapter dialogues are
about hobbies and weekend plans. The Korean you will actually
produce in your first month is about **your address, your card, a
delivery, an appointment, a broken thing in your flat.**

## The situations that actually come up

If you want a study list that maps onto your real life, it is
short. These are the encounters that repeat, in rough order of how
soon they hit you:

- **Ordering, paying, and the follow-up question** — for here or
  takeaway, do you have a point card, do you want a bag. The
  [restaurant system](/blog/korean-restaurant-how-it-works/) has its
  own script, and cafes run the
  [opposite one](/blog/korea-cafe-culture-guide/)
- **The convenience store counter** — bag, heating something up,
  topping up a transit card
- **Deliveries and the door** — the courier calling you, the
  entrance code, "leave it at the door"
- **Taxis and directions** — a destination, a landmark, "just here
  is fine"
- **A pharmacy or clinic** — describing a symptom well enough to be
  handed the right thing.
  [Which clinic to walk into](/blog/which-clinic-korea-department-guide/)
  is half the problem; saying what hurts is the other half
- **Your building** — the management office, a neighbour, a
  [leak or a repair](/blog/water-leak-korean-apartment/)
- **Anything with a landlord or an agent** — the highest-stakes
  Korean you will ever attempt, and the one to prepare for rather
  than improvise. What to ask is in the
  [renting guide](/blog/what-to-check-before-renting-seoul/)

That list is twenty-odd exchanges, not a language. Rehearsed, it
covers most of a normal week here.

## What actually moves your speaking

**Rehearse out loud, not in your head.** Reading a phrase and
saying it are different skills. The mouth needs the reps.

**Learn the replies, not just the questions.** For each situation,
prepare the two or three things they are most likely to say back.
That is what stops the freeze.

**Shrink your sentences.** Learners try to say the whole thought and
stall halfway. Say the short version. Short and delivered beats
complete and abandoned, every time.

**Get one situation to automatic before adding another.** Ordering
coffee should be boring before you take on the pharmacy.

**Bank the phrase that buys you time.** Knowing how to say "one
moment", "again please", "slowly please" turns a collapse into a
pause. These are the highest-value phrases in the language and
almost nobody drills them.

## The bit you cannot self-study

Reading, listening and vocabulary all work fine alone. Apps are
genuinely good at them.

**Speaking does not work that way.** You cannot hear your own
mistakes, you cannot know whether what you said landed as polite or
blunt, and you cannot practise being interrupted. Nor can you
generate the unexpected reply that is the actual failure point —
you know what you are about to say.

That is what a person is for. Not grammar explanations you could
have read; **someone to be the counter staff, say the unexpected
thing, and tell you what you actually sounded like.**

Which leaves the practical question of what kind of person:

- **A university language institute** is thorough and structured,
  and runs as a fixed timetable of classes — the lectures are
  excellent and the schedule is not negotiable
- **A conversation exchange partner** is free and sociable, and
  will almost never correct you, because correcting a friend is
  rude in any language
- **A tutor** is the one that fits around a job: your hours, your
  situations, and someone whose actual job is to tell you what you
  got wrong

Politeness level is the clearest example. The gap between fine and
faintly rude in Korean is a verb ending, and no app will ever tell
you that you have been slightly rude to a shopkeeper for six
months.

## Practise on the small stuff, get help with the big stuff

One rule worth adopting early: **the fastest progress comes from
needing the language** — but choose where you need it.

The coffee order, the convenience store, the taxi, the delivery
driver: practise on these. Low stakes, high repetition, and nobody
minds.

A contract, a hospital admission, a dispute with a landlord, a
deposit you might lose: **do not make these your practice ground.**
That is what our [anything-else concierge service](/etc) is for. We
will take the call in Korean and tell you in English what was
actually said.

## Get it sorted

The one-line version: **the problem is rehearsal, not vocabulary.
Pick the twenty situations that make up your week, drill the
replies rather than the questions, learn the phrases that buy you
time — and get a person for the speaking, because it is the one
part you genuinely cannot do alone.**
