The flat is fine. The location works, the rent works, and then you
notice the wallpaper has gone yellow above the radiator and there is a
tear in the floor covering by the kitchen door.

Most people assume that is simply the flat they are renting. In Korea
it often isn't. Between tenants, landlords here routinely replace both
— but it is a request, not a default, and the window to make it closes
the moment you sign.

## 도배 and 장판, and why they get replaced

Two words come up together so often that Koreans say them as a pair.

**도배 (dobae)** is wallpapering. Korean walls are papered rather than
painted, so a refresh means stripping and re-hanging paper across the
whole flat rather than rolling on a new coat.

**장판 (jangpan)** is the vinyl sheet flooring laid over the heated
floor. It comes in thicknesses — 1.8mm at the cheap end, 2.2mm and
above for something that feels solid underfoot and takes furniture
better.

Because both are relatively quick and priced by floor area, replacing
them between tenants is a normal cost of doing business for a landlord,
not a renovation. A flat that shows well rents faster. That is the
whole reason the request works.

## What you can reasonably ask for

First, adjust your expectations for the two separately. **Paper and
flooring are not equally likely to be replaced.** Wallpapering is
cheaper, faster and marks more visibly, so landlords redo it between
tenants fairly readily. Flooring is the more expensive job, and if the
existing vinyl is in decent shape a landlord will very often leave it
exactly where it is — and that is a normal answer, not a brush-off.

So ask for both, expect the paper, and treat new flooring as the thing
you have to actually make a case for.

You are on firm ground when there is a condition problem rather than a
taste problem:

| Reasonable ask | Weak ask |
|---|---|
| Vinyl that is **torn, lifting, or burned** | Vinyl in a colour you dislike |
| Wallpaper **stained, peeling, or smoke-yellowed** | Wallpaper that is simply plain |
| Paper damaged by **damp** (fix the cause too) | Wanting a feature wall |
| Flooring worn through at a doorway | Wanting a different material entirely |

Two details worth knowing before you agree to "we'll redo the paper":

- **Ask which paper.** 합지 (hapji) is plain paper — cheapest, marks
  easily, and a wipe takes the print off. 실크 (silk) is
  PVC-coated, wipeable, and lasts a tenancy properly. Landlords quote
  hapji by default. Asking for silk is a normal negotiation and
  sometimes costs you a small share of the difference.
- **Ask the flooring thickness in mm.** Jangpan is sold in grades —
  1.8mm, 2.2mm, 2.7mm and upward — and "new flooring" can mean any of
  them. Thicker is softer underfoot and takes furniture dents better,
  but it also sits between you and the underfloor heating, so it is a
  trade-off rather than a straight upgrade. Ask so that you know which
  one you are getting, not because a bigger number is automatically
  better.

If the wallpaper is damp-stained rather than just old, deal with the
cause before the cosmetics — new paper over a wet wall is back to
where it started within a season. That check belongs at the viewing,
and [mould behind the baseboard](/blog/mould-behind-baseboard-korea/)
covers what to look for.

## Say it before you sign

This is the part that decides everything, and it is not really about
wallpaper.

Before signing, your request is a **condition of the deal**. The
landlord wants a signed tenant. After signing, the same request is a
favour, and favours get postponed.

So put it in the contract. Korean lease agreements have a
**특약사항 (teukyaksahang)** section — special terms — near the end,
and it is the place where anything agreed verbally becomes
enforceable. A line there naming what will be replaced, and by when,
is worth more than any amount of goodwill in the viewing.

Be specific in it. "Wallpaper and flooring to be replaced" leaves the
grade and the timing open. Name the rooms, the material, and a date
that lands **before** your move-in.

## The order matters: papering first, cleaning last

Here is the sequencing detail that catches people out.

Papering and flooring are **construction work**. Stripping old paper,
mixing paste, cutting vinyl and trimming edges leaves dust, offcuts
and paste residue across the whole flat. That is normal and it is not
a sign anyone did a bad job.

Which means the clean has to come **after** the work, never before. A
flat cleaned on Tuesday and papered on Wednesday is a flat that needs
cleaning again on Thursday.

And this is where the bill quietly changes hands. The landlord pays
for the dobae and jangpan. The **move-in clean that the work makes
necessary is usually yours** — 입주청소 (ipjucheongso) is
conventionally the incoming tenant's cost, and getting the walls and
floors redone does not change that. It is a good trade, but it is a
trade rather than a free upgrade, so budget for it.

Guide ranges for the clean, as of 2026: a studio around 10 pyeong runs
around ₩250,000, a two-bedroom around 20 pyeong about ₩500,000, and
30 pyeong upward from ₩700,000. Condition moves
the number more than floor area does, and a flat that has just had
work done sits at the heavier end of its range rather than the lighter
one. The [cleaning page](/cleaning) has the current guide ranges.

## Who pays for what

| Item | Usually paid by |
|---|---|
| 도배 — new wallpaper, if agreed before signing | Landlord |
| 장판 — new vinyl flooring, if agreed before signing | Landlord |
| Upgrade from hapji to silk paper, or thicker vinyl | Sometimes split |
| 입주청소 — the move-in clean afterwards | **You** |
| Repairs after you move in | **Depends on the cause** |

That last row is not a dodge. Wear and failure of the flat's fixed
equipment — the boiler, the plumbing, a leak — is normally the
landlord's. Consumables, minor upkeep and anything damaged through use
are yours. Most disputes are really arguments about which of those two
a particular fault is, which is why the answer is a cause and not a
person.

Either way, a landlord who has just paid for paper and flooring has
shown they maintain the place. That is a reasonable relationship to
open with rather than to close.

One more thing for the far end of the tenancy. When you leave, you owe
the flat back in its handover condition minus normal wear. Fresh paper
at move-in raises what "handover condition" means, so photograph
everything on day one —
[what to photograph on move-in day](/blog/photograph-your-flat-move-in-day/)
walks through which shots actually matter when a deposit is being
argued over two years later.

## The Korean part

Four places this goes wrong for people renting in English:

1. **The ask itself.** It is usually made in person at the viewing or
   by phone to the agent, in Korean, in the same conversation as the
   rent. If you cannot make it there, it does not get made.
2. **The special-terms clause.** The contract is in Korean. A verbal
   yes that never reaches 특약사항 is not an agreement.
3. **Scheduling the work.** The previous tenant leaves, the paperers
   come, the cleaners come, your movers come — four parties, one
   week, and none of them coordinating with each other.
4. **Judging the result.** Whether the paper was hung properly, and
   whether you got the grade you asked for, is a conversation to have
   on the day rather than after your furniture is on top of it.
