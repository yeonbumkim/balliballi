There is no 911 in Korea — and in a real emergency, that's exactly the
kind of thing you don't want to be googling. Korea splits emergencies
across two numbers, both free, both 24/7, and both more
foreigner-friendly than most people assume. Save these now; read the
rest when you can.

## The two numbers that matter

**112 — Police.** Crime, theft, assault, harassment, traffic accidents
with a dispute, anything where you need officers. For a straightforward
bump between two cars, though, the police usually aren't the call you
need first — [here's who to ring after a car accident, and in what
order](/blog/rental-car-accident-korea/).

**119 — Fire & Ambulance.** Fire, medical emergencies, rescue —
including situations like being trapped in an elevator. One number
covers all of it; the dispatcher routes the right team.

If you're weighing up whether something is an emergency at all, we wrote
the whole ladder separately — **[convenience store, pharmacy, clinic,
then 119](/blog/sick-in-korea-medicine-day-or-night/)** — because most
midnight health problems stop at the first rung.

Both services handle foreign-language calls: dispatchers can bring an
**interpreter into the call three-way** (English and many other
languages). Don't hang up if the first voice is Korean — say "English,
please" and hold on. Speaking slowly and leading with your location
works better than perfect sentences.

## You can also text them

The part almost no foreigner knows: **both 112 and 119 accept SMS
reports.** Text your situation and location to the number just like a
phone contact — and you can attach photos or video (a threatening
situation, a fire, an accident scene). It exists for people who can't
speak safely (a domestic situation, an intruder in the home), for the
hearing-impaired — and it quietly solves the language problem too, since
a short English text with an address is easier for dispatch to translate
than a panicked call. There are also official apps (the government's
Emergency Ready app in English) with one-tap reporting, but plain SMS
needs no setup at all.

When texting, lead with what and where: *"Fire, apartment 302, 12
Itaewon-ro 34-gil, Yongsan-gu."* Address first if you only manage one
line.

## What happens when you call 119

Worth knowing in advance so it doesn't surprise you mid-crisis: the
**119 ambulance is a public service and free of charge** — there's no
bill for the ride. Paramedics take you to an appropriate emergency room,
and the hospital costs themselves are where insurance matters. And if the question is "it hurts, but is this
ER-worthy?" — **ask 119 itself.** The same number runs emergency medical
guidance: they will talk through the symptoms and tell you **which
hospital near you is open right now**, without dispatching an ambulance
you don't need.

A three-phrase Korean emergency kit, if you want one: **도와주세요**
(*dowajuseyo* — help me), **불이야** (*buriya* — fire!), **구급차
불러주세요** (*gugeupcha bulleojuseyo* — please call an ambulance). But
again — English plus an address gets the job done on both numbers.

## The support cast

| Number  | What it's for                                              |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1330** | Korea Travel Helpline — 24/7 tourist help & interpretation |
| **120**  | Seoul Dasan Call Center — city services, complaints, info (foreign-language support in working hours) |
| **1345** | Immigration Contact Center — visa/ARC questions, multilingual |
| **1339** | KDCA disease helpline — infectious disease, outbreak and travel-vaccination questions |

The one to remember alongside 112/119 is **1330**: round-the-clock,
**eight languages**, and happy to interpret or point you to the right
agency when the situation is urgent-ish but not sirens-urgent. It will
even **conference in 1345** (immigration) on a three-way call rather
than making you hang up and start again — which is the single most
useful thing any Korean helpline does for a foreigner.

One more thing your phone will do without asking: Korea's **emergency
alert texts (재난문자)** blast every handset in an affected area for
floods, extreme weather, and civil alerts — loudly, and in Korean. Don't
dismiss them unread; a ten-second pass through a translation app tells
you whether it's a heatwave advisory or something that needs your
attention right now.

## Know where you are

The weak point in every foreigner's emergency call is the address.
Korean addresses lead with the district and street (our
[transportation guide](/blog/seoul-transportation-guide/) covers the
system) — but in the moment, three shortcuts work:

- Every utility pole and building corner carries a **road-name address
  plate** — read it out, even in bad pronunciation.
- Subway exits, convenience stores and phone-booth-style emergency
  pillars are locatable landmarks — "GS25 next to exit 4, Itaewon
  Station" dispatches fine.
- Screenshot your home's Korean address **now** and keep it pinned in
  your notes and taped inside a kitchen cabinet. Two minutes today; it's
  the line you'll read aloud with shaking hands someday.
