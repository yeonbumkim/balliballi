School finishes in the middle of the afternoon. Your child wants to do something with
the rest of it, would rather it involved moving than sitting, and does not yet speak
Korean. Every website you open assumes all three of those are false.

A parent asked us a very specific version of this recently, and the honest answer
turned out to be the same one for most families around Seoul Foreign School in
Yeonhui-dong, Yongsan International School in Hannam-dong, and Lycée Français in
Seorae Village. So here it is properly.

## Why this is harder than it looks

Five structural facts, none of them obvious from outside.

### Korean academies sell months, not classes

The default is 월 단위 등록 — monthly enrolment on a fixed schedule, usually two or
three set days a week, paid whether or not your child turns up. **This is not a
starting position to negotiate from. It is simply how the sector is built**, and it
holds for private lessons as much as for group classes.

Two genuine exceptions:

- **Drop-in facilities** — climbing gyms, ice rinks, public courts, screen golf. You
  pay at the door, every time, and nothing is committed.
- **원데이 클래스 (one-day classes)** — pottery, cooking, craft. Good for a one-off,
  not for building a habit.

There is a third thing you will hear about, and it is worth understanding precisely.
A **회차권 (hoechagwon)** is a ticket for a set number of sessions rather than a
month. It exists, it is more common in some places than others, and it is worth
asking about — but treat a yes as a lucky break rather than the plan. Most academies
will say no, and a parent who arrives determined to pay per class often ends up with
nothing rather than with flexibility.

**The realistic lever is not the ticket book. It is 보강 — makeup classes.** Ask what
happens when your child misses a session, before you ask whether you can avoid paying
for one.

### An early weekday slot is empty, and that is your leverage

Korean elementary pupils typically do not reach an academy until 4pm or later, and
older children much later. Mid-afternoon on a weekday is, for most facilities, dead
time.

That is not a problem. It is an opening. A coach with an empty court at 3pm is far
more likely to agree to a one- or two-child private lesson, an off-peak rate, or a
flexible ticket than the same coach at 6pm. International school families are close
to the only customers who can fill that hour, and good venues know it.

The mistake is asking *"do you have a class at three?"* — the answer is no — instead
of *"can a coach teach my child privately at three?"*, which is often yes, and costs
less than you would guess.

### Language load varies enormously by activity

This is the part most guides get wrong. The barrier is not a constant; it scales with
how verbal the activity is.

**Low language load** — tennis, badminton, squash, table tennis, climbing, swimming,
fencing, archery, taekwondo, gymnastics, skating. These are taught by demonstration,
correction and repetition. A ten-year-old absorbs *racket back*, *step in*, *again*
within two sessions, and a coach with basic English is genuinely enough.

**High language load** — music theory, drama, coding, chess, debate, art critique,
and team sports that run on tactical instruction. Here you really do need an
English-speaking instructor.

So for a child who has no Korean yet, a racket sport or a climbing wall is close to
the ideal first activity. It builds a routine and a peer group without requiring
language first.

### Most academies have nowhere for you to sit

Korean 학원 culture is drop-and-go, and a classroom academy usually has a small
entryway and nothing else. Sports facilities are different — tennis academies,
climbing gyms, pools and rinks generally have a viewing area or a café, because
Korean parents watch too.

If waiting on site matters to you, filter towards sports centres over classroom
academies, and ask directly.

### Registration has friction

Bookings often run through Naver, a KakaoTalk channel, or a phone call in Korean.
Some places want a Korean phone number and a bank transfer rather than a card. None
of it is fatal, but it is why many parents give up at step one and default to
whatever their school offers.

One more thing nobody warns you about: once enrolled, **academies communicate through
KakaoTalk groups or a Korean parent app**. Being outside that loop is the most common
way an expat family misses a cancellation or a schedule change. Ask how you will be
contacted, and make sure you are actually in it.

## Ask about the shuttle before you ask about anything else

Many Korean academies run their own **shuttle bus** — a small bus that collects
children from fixed points, brings them in, and takes them home or on to the next
activity. That is why Korean parents can stack several activities into a weekday
afternoon without driving anywhere, and why a slightly further academy with a shuttle
can be far more practical than a closer one without.

**Whether it costs extra varies, so ask rather than assume.** Some academies fold it
into the monthly fee; others bill it separately, and a shuttle charge on top of
tuition is common enough that it should be part of the number you compare.

Four questions, in this order: does a shuttle run at all, is there a stop near you,
is it included or extra, and does it drop home afterwards as well as collect. Routes
are fixed and sometimes full, so *"we have a bus"* and *"we can collect your child"*
are two different answers — and a fifth question is worth adding if you are enrolling
for a term: does it run during school holidays.

## Holidays: do the arithmetic before you rule out monthly fees

Worrying about paying a full month and losing half of it to holidays is reasonable.
It is also worth checking, because the answer is often better than it feels.

Korea's autumn holidays for 2026 fall like this: **Chuseok 24–27 September**,
**National Foundation Day 3 October with a substitute holiday on Monday the 5th**,
and **Hangeul Day on Friday 9 October**. Depending on which weekday your activity
lands on, the national calendar may barely touch you at all.

What will interrupt you is your own school's calendar. International schools often
schedule a longer autumn break around Chuseok, plus professional development and
conference days that match no national calendar. So:

1. Open your school's calendar and count the sessions you would actually miss.
2. Ask about **보강 (bogang)** — makeup classes — before you ask about anything else.
   Many academies offer them, and they are most willing for an off-peak slot they
   were going to leave empty anyway.
3. If the misses pile up and no makeup is offered, that is the moment to look at a
   drop-in facility for those weeks instead — a climbing gym or a rink you pay at
   the door — rather than to fight the monthly model.

A month you partly miss, with makeup sessions, usually still beats an arrangement you
could not actually get. Do the sum rather than assuming.

## What things cost

Fees are quoted per month and priced by how many times a week the child attends. The
same academy will have a 주2회 and a 주3회 price, and they are not proportional —
three days a week is often better value per session.

| Type | Typical shape |
| ---- | ------------- |
| Group academy class, 2–3× a week | Monthly tuition; the cheapest route per session |
| Semi-private sports lesson, 2–3 children | Mid-range, and usually the sweet spot for value |
| One-to-one coaching, 40–60 min | The upper end, but it solves language and scheduling at once |
| Drop-in facility — climbing, skating, public courts | Day passes; municipal facilities are cheaper still |
| Instrument lesson, one-to-one | Varies widely with the instructor's background |

Confirm current rates directly — these are shapes, not prices.

Before you sign anything, establish what the monthly fee covers (shuttle, equipment
and facility use are each sometimes bundled and sometimes billed on top), what is
extra (uniforms,
grading tests, competition entry, recital fees), how mid-month joining is pro-rated,
and what happens to your money if you stop partway through.

## Racket sports: the strong first choice

**Tennis** is the most available racket sport in the city. Indoor academies exist
across Seoul and increasingly take individual bookings, and off-peak weekday
afternoons are the easiest time to secure a coach. Many have ball machines, so a
lesson can be paired with practice time. Racket loan for the first few sessions is
normal — ask before buying one.

**Badminton** is taken seriously here, and there is real public infrastructure,
including free municipal courts listed on district office websites. The catch is that
public courts are for playing rather than teaching, so you would arrange a coach
separately, and proper indoor shoes are usually required for entry.

**Table tennis** is widely available, very cheap, and underrated for children —
fast, indoor, weather-proof and excellent for hand-eye coordination. Dedicated
children's coaching is thinner on the ground than for tennis, so expect to ask around.

**Squash** is much rarer and mostly attached to private fitness clubs. Worth asking
about; do not build a plan around it.

**Padel and pickleball** are the newest arrivals and the answer to "something we have
not tried" that is still a racket sport. Facilities are mostly new, growing fast, and
the coaching culture is more casual and beginner-friendly than tennis.

## Something genuinely new

**Bouldering** may be the single best fit for these constraints. It is drop-in by
design, several well-regarded gyms sit in Yeonnam-dong and Mapo with English-speaking
staff, the sport is close to language-free, and there is usually a lounge for parents.
A beginner can be climbing within the first hour. Look for a gym running a dedicated
children's class rather than one that merely permits children — supervision and wall
grades differ.

**Fencing.** Korea is a genuine fencing power and the club scene reflects it.
Drill-based, highly physical, low language load. Children who like the idea of a
racket often like this more.

**Archery.** The Korean sport, taught extremely well. Which facilities take children
varies, so this one needs individual checking.

**Ice skating and hockey.** Rinks run on session tickets, which suits pay-per-visit
families neatly.

**Taekwondo.** The obvious one, and obvious for good reason — structured, welcoming
to beginners, and the belt system gives visible progress in a country where a new
arrival may feel behind at everything else.

**K-pop dance.** Studios in Hongdae and Mapo increasingly run youth classes, some in
English, often sold as class packs.

## Swimming, and the thing about public pools

Two routes. District sports centres are the cheap option, run terms by the month, and
are heavily oversubscribed — popular children's slots are allocated by **lottery
(추첨)** on a fixed date each month. That makes it a calendar problem rather than a
queueing one, and missing the date costs you the term. Private swim academies cost
more, have space, and will usually take a beginner at any point.

## Music

For a child without Korean, one-to-one instrument lessons are the realistic route —
piano, guitar, drums or violin with an instructor who has enough English. Group music
classes assume a shared language and move quickly.

Worth considering: **Korean traditional instruments.** Janggu, gayageum or samulnori
are taught largely by imitation and rhythm, which lowers the language barrier
considerably. Very few expat children try it, and the ones who do tend to remember it.

## School or home: where to anchor

This quietly determines everything. Seoul Foreign School sits in Yeonhui-dong,
Seodaemun-gu. If you live in Yongsan — Seobinggo, Ichon, Hannam — that is a
meaningful drive, and doing it twice in an afternoon is a different commitment from
walking round the corner.

**Anchor near school** when the activity starts straight after the bell. Pick
somewhere within ten or fifteen minutes of the gate — Yeonhui-dong, Yeonnam-dong,
Sinchon or Mapo — so your child goes directly, you drive up once, and you go home
together. There is no time to go home first anyway.

**Anchor near home** when the activity is in the evening or at a weekend.

For YISS families the equivalent clusters are Hannam-dong, Itaewon and Oksu; for LFS,
Seorae Village and Banpo. The logic is identical: find the venue sitting between the
school gate and your front door.

## Four questions worth asking in Korean

Paste these into a KakaoTalk message or read them down the phone. They cut to the
four things that actually decide it.

- **결석하면 보강이 되나요?** — If we miss a session, is there a makeup class?
- **영어로 지도 가능한 코치님이 계신가요?** — Is there a coach who can teach in English?
- **평일 오후 3시에 개인 레슨이 가능한가요?** — Is a private lesson possible at 3pm on a weekday?
- **학부모 대기 공간이 있나요?** — Is there a waiting area for parents?

Four yeses and you have found it. A fifth question — **회차권으로 결제할 수
있나요?**, can I pay per session rather than monthly — is worth asking, but expect
the answer to be no. Then ask for a **체험 수업 (chehum sueop)** — a
trial class. It is a standard offer, often free, and asking is the expected first
step rather than a cheek. Take it up at more than one place: what you are testing is
whether a coach can hold your child's attention without a shared language, and that
varies enormously between people at otherwise identical academies.

## The Korean part

None of this is difficult. It is just conducted in Korean, by phone, during the hours
you are at work — and the information that decides it is not on any website. It is
what the front desk says when you walk in.

The time goes on the twelve phone calls, the Naver listings that never mention
whether anyone speaks English, the KakaoTalk channel that replies in three days, and
the discovery on call nine that the place with the perfect schedule has no parking
and nowhere to wait.
