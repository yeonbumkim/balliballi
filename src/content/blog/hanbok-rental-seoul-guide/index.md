Every photo you've seen of Gyeongbokgung — the palace courtyards
full of people in flowing silk — has a boring secret: almost nobody
owns those clothes. They rented them twenty minutes earlier, a block
from the gate, for about the price of two coffees an hour.

A traveller on r/KoreaTravel put the real question better than any
guide does. They wanted a **more authentic** hanbok, they wrote,
rather than *"those houses where they give you a hoop skirt and call
it a day."* They also wondered whether a **modern** hanbok could be
rented, and whether the palaces would accept one.

Both halves of that have an answer, and the second one is written
down by the government. Here it is, along with the prices, what the
expensive tier actually buys, and the one thing you should not agree
to at the counter.

## The deal that makes it worth it

Here's the hook that built the whole industry: **wear a hanbok and
the royal palaces let you in free.** Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung
and the other Joseon palaces waive admission for anyone in Korean
traditional dress — a government campaign that turned into a
permanent feature of Seoul tourism. Palace tickets are cheap anyway
(a few thousand won), so the real win isn't the savings — it's that
the rule filled the palaces with color and made dressing up the
normal thing to do rather than the touristy thing to do.

One planning note that catches people constantly: **Gyeongbokgung
closes on Tuesdays** (Changdeokgung closes Mondays). Check the day
before you build your outfit around it.

## What counts as a hanbok — the official version

This is the part almost nothing in English tells you, and it settles
the modern-hanbok question outright.

The Royal Palaces authority publishes an actual guideline for the free
entry, and the headline is generous: **생활한복 — modern or "lifestyle"
hanbok — counts, as well as traditional.** You do not have to be in
full Joseon costume.

What it will not accept is **half an outfit**:

| Accepted | Not accepted |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional hanbok | Jeans with a jeogori on top |
| Modern / lifestyle hanbok | Hanbok trousers with a T-shirt |
| A wrap-front jeogori **even without the 고름 ties** | A jeogori you pull on over your head like a T-shirt |
| Hanbok trousers in the proper 사폭 cut | — |
| 모시 ramie summer wear, if it otherwise fits the guideline | — |

The logic is consistent once you see it: **the shape has to be
hanbok, top and bottom.** A wrap-front jacket qualifies even if the
ties are missing, because the construction is right; a hanbok-print
T-shirt does not, because it is a T-shirt.

Free entry needs no reservation and applies to residents and visitors
alike, day or night.

**And the calendar catch:** Gyeongbokgung closes on **Tuesdays**,
Changdeokgung on Mondays. Check the day before you build an outfit
around it.

## Hoop skirt, or layers

Now the first half of that traveller's question — how to tell the
costume shops from the good ones without having been inside.

The cheap end achieves volume with **a hooped petticoat under a
shiny polyester skirt.** It photographs as a bell: a hard, even curve
with a visible ring at the hem. The better shops build the same
silhouette with **layered underskirts**, which falls softly and moves
when you do. One commenter on that thread picked their shop precisely
because *"they really use layers to poof up your hanbok"* — which is
the single most useful sentence in the whole discussion.

You can check this before you go. **Look at the shop's Instagram, not
its price list.** Everything you need to judge is visible in their own
photographs: whether the fabrics are matte or shiny, whether the
palettes are subtle or fluorescent, and whether the skirts hang or
flare. It is the same trick that works for
[choosing a hair stylist here](/blog/haircut-in-korea-prices-salons/) —
the portfolio tells you what the description will not.

## Book the good shops ahead — properly ahead

The strip by Gyeongbokgung station has **thirty to fifty rental
shops**, and more spread from Insadong up towards Bukchon. Walk-ins
are the norm there and the reason the strip exists.

**The premium shops do not work that way.** Travellers in that thread
turned up at two well-known ones and were turned away for having no
reservation; one was told to book **at least a week in advance**. If
you have a specific shop in mind — the sort you found on Instagram —
assume a week, not a morning.

What the higher tier bought, in one traveller's account at around
**₩50,000**: the hanbok, **hair styling included**, hair accessories,
a bag to carry with the outfit, traditional shoes, and lockers for
your own things while you are out. That is the honest comparison to
make against a ₩15,000 rack rental — not the dress alone.

Names that come up repeatedly in that thread, offered as leads to
check rather than as our recommendations: **Princess Hanbok**
(Insadong), **Studio KJD**, **MOA Hanbok** and **Bomeun Hanbok**. We
have not rented from any of them.

## Do not leave your passport

One traveller asked the question nobody answered properly: some
rental shops ask you to **leave your passport as collateral**, and
they were not comfortable with that.

They were right not to be. **Your passport is the document that
proves you are lawfully in the country**, it is issued by your
government rather than owned by you, and it should not spend an
afternoon in a drawer behind a shop counter while you are three
palaces away.

You are not obliged to agree, and you have alternatives to offer:

- **Another photo ID.** A driving licence or a residence card is the
  usual substitute, and shops generally accept one.
- **A cash or card deposit** instead of a document.
- **A different shop.** There are thirty of them on that street.

Say it plainly and early — before you have chosen an outfit, not
after you are half dressed. A shop that will not take anything but a
passport is telling you something about how it treats customers.

## How rental actually works

The shops cluster exactly where you'd hope: **around Gyeongbokgung's
gates, Anguk station, and the edges of Bukchon and Insadong.** The
process is the same everywhere:

1. Pick a hanbok off the racks (staff will help match jeogori
   jacket to skirt/pants — trust them, they do this all day).
2. Add options: braided hair styling with ribbons, hairpins,
   handbags, a padded vest in winter.
3. Leave your clothes and bag in a locker, take a claim tag, and
   walk out the door in the Joseon dynasty.

Rental windows run by the hour, and **4 hours is the standard
tourist package** — enough for a palace plus Bukchon without
rushing. Day rentals exist for making a whole itinerary of it.

## What it costs (as of 2026)

| Option | Typical price |
| ------ | ------------- |
| Basic hanbok, 4 hours | ₩10,000–20,000 |
| Premium / theme hanbok (better fabric, embroidery), 4h | ₩20,000–40,000 |
| Premium shop, styling and accessories included | Around ₩50,000 |
| Full-day rental | Roughly double the 4-hour rate |
| Hair styling add-on | ₩5,000–10,000 |

The tier difference is visible in photos: the basic racks are shiny
polyester in loud colors, the premium racks are matte fabrics and
subtler palettes. If the photos are the point of the day, the
premium tier is the best ₩15,000 upgrade in Seoul. Walk-ins are fine
on weekdays; **reserve ahead for weekends and cherry-blossom or
autumn peak season**, when the popular shops run out of the good
sizes by mid-morning.

And one detour for the hooked: if you'd rather own one, the
**Dongmyo secondhand market** (Line 1/6) turns up barely-worn hanbok
for around what a premium rental costs — Koreans often buy a fine
hanbok for a single family event, and those end up here in
near-perfect condition.

## The photo route

The classic circuit, tuned by ten thousand Instagram accounts:
start at **Gyeongbokgung** early (the courtyards are emptiest right
at opening), then walk east into **Bukchon Hanok Village** for the
tiled-roof alley shots — go quietly; people live there — and finish
down through **Insadong** for tea and souvenirs while still dressed.
Morning light beats afternoon at the palace, and weekdays beat
weekends by a mile. In summer, book the earliest slot you can:
hanbok layers plus a Seoul August afternoon is a commitment.

Two upgrades for repeat visitors: Gyeongbokgung runs **seasonal
night openings** a few times a year, and hanbok under the lit-up
eaves is a completely different photo set — slots sell out, so it
takes planning. And if your Korea trip goes beyond Seoul, **Jeonju
Hanok Village** is the national capital of the hanbok stroll, with
its own rental streets and a full day of alleys to use them in.

Hungry afterward? Gwangjang Market and its street-food alleys are a
short ride east, and Insadong is full of tea houses that will not
blink at a table of people in hanbok. If it is your first sit-down
meal here, the drawers, the call bell and the free banchan are
explained in
[how Korean restaurants work](/blog/korean-restaurant-how-it-works/).
