Three students moved into one of our share houses on a Saturday. Before they signed,
they asked us the question every new arrival asks: **can you put toilet paper down a
Korean toilet?**

We said yes, which is the right answer.

The toilet blocked the next day.

Both of those things are true at once, and the gap between them is what this article
is about.

## Stop flushing

If the water is rising instead of draining, this is the only thing that matters in
the next ten seconds.

**Do not flush again to see whether it clears.** It will not. A second flush adds
another six litres to a bowl that is already full, and that is the moment a blocked
toilet becomes a flooded bathroom — which in a Korean building means the ceiling of
the flat below yours, and a bill that is an order of magnitude larger than the one
you were trying to avoid.

If it is already at the rim, **lift the cistern lid and close the water valve** — the
tap on the pipe behind or below the toilet, turned clockwise. That stops the tank
refilling and takes the next flush off the table entirely.

Then leave it twenty minutes. Water finds its way past a partial blockage on its own
more often than people expect, and a bowl that has dropped an inch on its own will
usually take a plunger well.

## Can you flush toilet paper in Korea?

**Yes.** Modern Korean plumbing takes toilet paper, and public toilets have been
signed for flushing it rather than binning it for years now. The bins you still see
beside the toilet in some older buildings are a leftover from when the advice was the
opposite, and in a few genuinely old buildings they are still there for a reason.

Two things qualify the yes, and they are what caught our students out.

**Older pipes are narrower, and a three-storey house is not a new tower.** The same
amount of paper that vanishes in a 2020 apartment can sit in a 1990s riser.

**"A normal amount" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.** Flush as you go rather
than building a wad and sending it all at once. The failure is almost never a single
sheet too many — it is volume arriving together.

## What actually blocks a Korean toilet

In rough order of how often it is the answer:

- **Wet wipes (물티슈)** — including every one sold as "flushable". They do not break
  up in water; they are held together by fibres designed not to. This is the single
  most common cause, and Korean households use a lot of them.
- **Kitchen towel and facial tissue.** Both feel like toilet paper and neither
  disintegrates like it. Toilet paper is engineered to fall apart wet. Tissue is
  engineered not to.
- **Sanitary products, cotton pads, cotton buds, dental floss, hair.**
- **Cooking oil poured down it**, which does not block the toilet so much as line the
  pipe it drains into until something else catches.
- **Something dropped in.** Phones, toys, a bottle cap, a cleaning gel that came
  loose. This one does not respond to a plunger at all and needs a hand or an auger.

## The ₩3,000 tool that clears most of them

A plunger. In Korean it is a **뚫어뻥** — the word is a brand name that became the
noun, and every Daiso has one for a few thousand won. Buy it before you need it; the
one thing you cannot do at eleven at night with a full bowl is go shopping.

Using it properly matters more than people think:

- **There has to be enough water to cover the rubber cup.** Plunging air does
  nothing. If the bowl is nearly empty, add water rather than flushing for it.
- **Push down slowly the first time.** The first stroke is to expel the air under the
  cup. Ramming it drives that air back at you along with the contents of the bowl.
- **Then pump firmly, and keep the seal.** The pull is doing as much work as the
  push — you are trying to rock the blockage loose, not force it down.
- **Fifteen or twenty strokes, then stop and look.** If the level has dropped at all,
  repeat. If nothing has moved after two rounds, it is not a plunger job.

If the blockage is soft and organic, **a bucket of hot tap water poured in from waist
height** sometimes finishes what the plunger started — the height gives it force the
cistern cannot. Hot, not boiling: boiling water can crack a porcelain bowl, and a
cracked bowl is a new toilet.

**Do not pour chemical drain cleaner into a toilet.** It is made for a drain full of
hair, it sits in the trap doing very little to paper or wipes, and it turns the next
plunge into a caustic splash-back. The
[same bottle loses in a bathroom sink](/blog/unclog-bathroom-sink-korea/), for
related reasons.

## When to stop and call someone

- **The plunger has had two proper goes and nothing has moved.**
- **You know something solid went in.** A plunger pushes it further down the pipe,
  which turns a ₩70,000 retrieval into a much larger job.
- **Water is coming up somewhere else** — the shower drain gurgling when you flush,
  or the floor drain backing up. That is not your toilet. That is the line your
  toilet shares with the rest of the building, and it is not yours to fix.
- **It has blocked more than once in a month.** Something is wrong further down, and
  clearing it each time is paying for the same job repeatedly.

## Ask what it was before the plumber leaves

Here is the part that comes from our own weekend, and it is the reason this section
exists at all.

Our plumber came out the same day, cleared it, and left. **We still do not know what
was in there** — nobody was standing over him, he did not say, and we did not ask.

That sounds like a small thing. It is not. Without that answer, nothing changes in the
house, and the next blockage is a coin flip. If it was wipes, three people need to
know today. If it was volume, the advice is completely different. If it was the pipe
rather than anything anyone put in it, then it will happen again no matter what
anyone does, and the landlord needs to know rather than the tenants.

**One question, asked before he packs up, decides which.**

If your Korean will not carry it, hold your phone up with this on the screen:

> **뭐가 막고 있었어요? 사진 있으세요?**
> *What was blocking it? Do you have a photo?*

> **다음에 안 막히려면 뭘 조심해야 돼요?**
> *What should we avoid so it doesn't happen again?*

Plumbers are generally happy to answer this. They pulled it out; they know. The
reason you do not get told is almost always that nobody asked.

## Who pays, if you rent

The usual understanding — and it is an understanding rather than a rule, so your
contract and your landlord may see it differently:

- **What went down the toilet is the tenant's.** Wipes, an object, a wad of paper.
- **The pipe itself is the landlord's.** Roots, a collapsed line, a shared riser, or
  a toilet that blocks every few weeks with normal use.

The line between them is exactly the thing you cannot prove after the plumber has
gone — which is the second reason to ask him what it was, and to keep the answer in
writing. The same logic runs through
[who pays for a leak](/blog/water-leak-korean-apartment/) and
