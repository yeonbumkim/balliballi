Korea runs an enormous number of classes. Tennis at six in the morning,
pottery on a Saturday, climbing, piano, swimming, cooking, table tennis in a
basement with a former national-team coach in it.

Almost none of it is legible from the outside. A venue's page tells you the
name and an address. It rarely tells you **whether a foreigner can join, what
language the class runs in, how long a session lasts, whether you can try it
once**, or whether a ten-year-old with no Korean would be fine there.

So people give up at the search stage, which is the wrong stage to give up at.
Most of these places will take you. They just have no reason to say so in a
language you read.

## What the listings do not tell you

Five things decide whether a class works for you, and Korean listings are
silent on four of them.

| What you need to know | What a listing usually says |
|---|---|
| Is there an English option? | Nothing |
| How long is a session? | Sometimes |
| Can I pay for one class, or is it monthly? | Monthly, implied, never stated |
| Is there a trial? | Rarely, and often only by phone |
| Will you take a child with no Korean? | Nothing at all |

That last one is not hostility. It is that nobody has ever asked them, so
there is no policy to publish.

## The honest answer about language

**Most classes run in Korean.** Not some. Most. A venue that advertises
English teaching is unusual, and it charges for it.

The useful distinction is not English versus Korean. It is **how much of the
lesson is language at all.**

- **Sport is the lowest barrier.** A coach who can demonstrate a forehand
  does not need to explain it, and a beginner copying a demonstration is
  doing the same thing a Korean beginner is doing. Tennis, badminton, table
  tennis, climbing and swimming all work this way.
- **Music is workable.** Notation is international, and a piano teacher
  spends most of the hour pointing at keys.
- **Anything conversational is not.** Drama, debate, most art classes with a
  theory element, tutoring of any kind.

Somebody who has lived in Korea a while already knows this instinctively —
you pick up enough of the lesson to follow along, and your body does the
rest. Somebody in their first month does not know it yet, and assumes the
door is closed.

  booking is possible

  taxi driver or pasted into a map app

And what is still unknown, marked as unknown. Two of those eight had fees we
could not confirm without a phone call, and the sheet says so rather than
guessing.

## Then we call, and then we book

The sheet is the half you could theoretically do yourself with enough time
and enough Korean. The other half is the phone.

Four questions get asked, in Korean, every time:

1. **Can a child with no Korean join this class?** (The answer is usually
   yes, and often surprised.)
2. **Can we pay per session, or is it monthly enrolment?** There is a
   specific way of asking this that unlocks pay-as-you-go where it exists —
   it is covered in [hagwon classes in Seoul](/blog/hagwon-seoul-sport-music-classes/).
3. **Is there a trial lesson, and what does it cost?**
4. **What does the student need to bring?**

Then the booking itself: the slot, the name, the phone number the venue will
call, and the message afterwards telling you where the door is.

## The Korean part

Four places this breaks down, and they are all phone-shaped.

1. **Finding the venue at all.** The search that works is in Korean —
   `연희동 테니스 레슨`, not "tennis lessons near me" — and the results that
   matter are on Naver rather than Google.
2. **The sign-up page.** Most venues take bookings by phone or KakaoTalk
   channel, and a Korean phone number is often required to finish.
3. **The questions that decide it.** Whether a foreigner can join, whether
   one session can be bought, whether a trial exists — none of it is written
   down, so somebody has to ask.
4. **The first day.** What to bring, which entrance, whether indoor shoes are
   included, and who to tell if the child is going to be late.

Nothing there is difficult. All of it is Korean, on the phone, during working
hours, which is exactly the combination that keeps people from starting.
