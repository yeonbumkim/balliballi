A message came in today. Someone had a padlock on their suitcase and no key —
lost, somewhere between one country and another, in the way those things go.
Could we send someone to open it?

**Yes. Easily.** We know locksmiths, they answer the phone, and one of them
would have been at the door within the hour.

**We said no, and sent a shopping link instead.** This page is why, because
the reasoning is more useful than the answer.

## What it would have cost to do it our way

Start with our own price list, because it is published and we are not going
to pretend otherwise. [Our locksmith page](/locksmith) says a standard lockout
opening runs **₩50,000–100,000**, depending on the lock and the time of day.

That is a fair price and we stand behind it. **A call-out has a floor** — a
person stops what they are doing, travels across a city, and arrives with
tools. The number is mostly the journey, not the work, and it does not get
smaller because your problem is small.

Now look at what was actually being bought for it.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The job** | Cut one padlock |
| **The tool** | A bolt cutter |
| **The skill** | None |
| **The time** | Ten seconds |
| **The call-out** | **₩50,000–100,000** |

**There is no version of that where the customer wins.** At the top of the
range you are paying more than a lot of suitcases are worth. You could buy a
replacement case and throw the locked one away and still be ahead, which is an
absurd sentence to have to write about a lock the size of a thumb.

So we did not send anyone. **The fee we would have earned on that job is
money we decided not to make**, and this is the part of the business that
nobody writes down: the easiest thing in the world is to say yes.

## What we sent instead

Three options, cheapest first. Try them in this order.

### 1. Look at the lock before you cut it

**Thirty seconds, and it might save the padlock entirely.**

Check the face of the lock for a **TSA** mark or a small red diamond, and
check whether there is a key slot on the underside that does not match your
own key. Travel padlocks sold for flights are built so that airport security
can open them with a master key rather than cutting them off.

That does not mean a hardware shop can help — they will not have the master —
but a **luggage shop or a luggage repair counter** sometimes can, and it is
worth one phone call before you destroy anything. Department stores with a
luggage floor are the likeliest place to ask.

**And one thing worth knowing even after this is over:** a padlock on a soft
suitcase is not security. It is a deterrent against a casual hand, and the zip
it is attached to is the weakest part of the whole bag. Pack accordingly, and
do not put anything in there you would be sorry to lose.

### 2. Buy a bolt cutter

**This is the answer in almost every case.** A small bolt cutter — the kind
sold for cutting wire and light chain — goes through a suitcase padlock shackle
without any technique at all.

The one we sent was a **200mm (8-inch) mini bolt cutter at ₩4,660**, with
nearly a thousand reviews. The cheapest one on the page will do, because you
are cutting a few millimetres of soft brass, once.

**Buy the small size.** The same search offers a 600mm two-handed cutter at
₩17,990 — four times the price, for a tool built to cut rebar and chain, which
you will never use again and cannot get into a drawer. The 200mm is the right
tool here precisely because the job is small.

### 3. If it is urgent, walk to a 철물점

Overnight delivery is not fast enough when the flight is tomorrow morning.

**철물점 (*cheolmulcheom*) is the neighbourhood hardware shop**, and there is
one near you — usually a narrow, crowded place with tools hanging outside,
run by somebody who has been there for twenty years. They have bolt cutters.
They will cut it.

**Around ₩20,000** is the number we gave, and expect it to be less a
transaction than a favour. **Bring the suitcase** — this is the one condition
that matters. A shop will cut a lock in front of you in ten seconds; almost
none of them will come to your flat to do it, and the moment somebody has to
travel you are back at call-out prices.

Say this:

> **이것 좀 잘라 주실 수 있을까요?**
> *Igeot jom jallа jusil su isseulkkayo?* — "Could you cut this for me?"

If the shop is busy, point at the lock and hold up the suitcase. It is not a
sentence anyone will need repeating.

**Why this works and a call-out does not:** you are going to the tool instead
of paying the tool to come to you. The journey is the expensive part, and the
journey is now yours and it is five minutes.

**And we did not leave them to it.** We found a hardware shop near their
address, offered to ring it and ask whether the owner would do it, and said
what we thought it should cost. **That part is free and it is the part we are
actually for** — the Korean phone call, not the dispatch fee.

## So when is a locksmith the right call?

We are not telling you locksmiths are overpriced. We arrange them, we publish
what they cost, and we think the price is right **for the job they are
actually for.**

| Call one | Do not call one |
|---|---|
| You are **outside your home** and cannot get in | A padlock on something you are holding |
| The **keypad lock is dead**, or the door will not open with the right code | A lock you could carry to a shop |
| Somebody — or a pet — **is shut inside a room** | Anything a ₩5,000 tool solves in ten seconds |
| You need the **lock replaced or recoded** after moving in | |

The distinction is not how hard the lock is. **It is whether the lock is
attached to a building.** A door cannot come to the tool, so the tool has to
come to the door, and that journey is what you are paying for.

If it is a door rather than a suitcase, we have written both of the common
versions: [locked out of the flat
entirely](/blog/locked-out-in-seoul-real-callout/), and
[shut out of a room inside your own
home](/blog/locked-inside-room-korea/), which is usually solved in about a
minute with a paperclip.

## The part this is really about

We turned down a job today, and the honest reason is not that we are generous.

**It is that sending someone would have been the easy answer, and the wrong
one.** We would have been paid, the customer would have been annoyed in a week
when they realised, and they would have been right.
