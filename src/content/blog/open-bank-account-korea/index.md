A Korean bank account is the hinge that everything else turns on.
Your salary needs one. Your phone contract wants one. The shopping
apps want to verify one. And you cannot get one on your first
afternoon here, which catches almost everybody out.

Here is what it actually takes, and the one detail that surprises
people even after the account is open.

## What you need to bring

For a normal account, in practice:

- **Your Alien Registration Card (외국인등록증, ARC)** — this is the
  main gate. It arrives weeks after you apply, and until it does,
  most doors are shut
- **Your passport**
- **A Korean phone number in your own name** — needed for the
  banking app, one-time passwords and identity checks later
- **Something showing why you are here** — an employment contract,
  a certificate of enrolment, a lease. Not always asked for at the
  counter, but see the limit section below, where it matters a great
  deal
- **An address** you can state, matching your ARC if possible

**Before the ARC arrives**, some banks will open a restricted
account on a passport alone, and some will not. It varies by bank
and by branch, so it is worth asking rather than assuming — but do
not build a plan on it.

## Your visa decides how easy this is

Not every foreign resident is in the same position, and it is worth
knowing which group you are in before you queue:

| Status | Where you stand |
| ------ | --------------- |
| **F-4, F-5, F-6** (overseas Korean, permanent, marriage) | Effectively the same as a Korean customer |
| **Long-stay work and study** — E-7, E-9, F-2, D-2 | Accounts open at most banks, with documents |
| **D-4** (language study) | Often a limited account only, and more paperwork |
| **Short stay** — C-3 and similar | No ARC, so in practice no ordinary account |

The dividing line is the ARC, which is why the sequence below
matters so much.

## The chicken-and-egg, drawn out

This is the sequence that traps new arrivals, and it is worth
seeing whole:

1. **Most services want a Korean phone number** in your name
2. **A postpaid phone plan usually wants a bank account** to bill
3. **The bank wants the ARC**
4. **The ARC takes weeks**

The way through is to start with a **prepaid SIM**, which needs
neither, and treat the postpaid plan and the good bank account as
things that arrive later. We laid the same chain out from the
shopping side in the
[Coupang guide](/blog/coupang-korea-shopping-guide/), where it stops
people at checkout.

## The limited account nobody warns you about

Here is the part that is genuinely surprising, and the reason
people think their new account is broken.

**New accounts in Korea are usually opened as a limited account
(한도계좌, *hando gyejwa*)** — fully working for receiving money,
but with **low daily caps on transfers and ATM withdrawals.**

This is not aimed at foreigners. It is an anti-fraud measure, built
after years of voice-phishing scams that ran through freshly opened
accounts. Everybody gets it, Koreans included.

Money **comes in** fine — salary, transfers, refunds. Money **goes
out** slowly, and the ceilings are lower than people expect:

| Channel | Typical daily cap |
| ------- | ----------------- |
| **Online banking and ATM** | **₩1,000,000** |
| **At the counter, in branch** | **₩3,000,000** |

*Figures as of 2026-08. Banks apply their own variations, so confirm
yours at the counter rather than assuming.*

Look at that first row against a Korean rental deposit and the
problem is obvious. **A ₩10,000,000 deposit does not fit through a
₩1,000,000 daily online limit**, and finding that out the morning
of the signing is a genuinely bad day.

**If you know a large transfer is coming, deal with the limit
first.**

## Lifting the limit

You lift it by giving the bank a reason to believe you are a normal
customer with normal reasons to move money. Typically that means
bringing **evidence of income or of settled life**, such as:

- An **employment contract** or a **certificate of employment**
  (재직증명서)
- **Payslips** (급여명세서), or a few months of salary landing in the
  account
- A **health insurance qualification certificate**
  (건강보험 자격득실 확인서) — the one nobody thinks of, and one of
  the cleanest proofs that you are employed here
- A **certificate of enrolment** and tuition receipts if you are a
  student
- A **lease**, or utility bills in your name
- Sometimes simply **time** — an account used normally for a few
  months earns its limits back

Take the documents to the branch. This is a counter errand, not an
app one.

## Which bank

The large banks all handle foreign customers; they differ in how
much of it happens in English.

- **Hana (formerly KEB Hana)** has the deepest history with foreign
  customers and foreign exchange, and is the most commonly
  recommended starting point
- **Woori, Shinhan, KB Kookmin and NH** are all widely used, and
  branches in areas with many foreign residents — Itaewon, around
  the universities, the industrial districts — are far more used to
  the paperwork than a random neighbourhood branch
- **Pick the branch, not just the bank.** A branch that opens
  foreign accounts weekly will do in twenty minutes what an
  unfamiliar branch turns into an afternoon
- **The Seoul Global Center and the foreign workers' support centres
  keep lists** of branches used to foreign customers. Asking them
  first is faster than walking into three banks

Worth a look while you are choosing: several banks run **accounts
aimed at foreign customers** with better terms on currency exchange
and overseas remittance than their standard products. If you will
be sending money home regularly, that difference compounds.

## Two things changed in 2025

Both are recent enough that a lot of the advice circulating still
predates them.

**A mobile Alien Registration Card now works at the bank.** Since
**March 2025**, the digital version of your ARC is accepted for
opening an account at a growing list of banks. The list started
small and has been expanding, so ask your bank what it accepts
today rather than working from a list.

**Opening without visiting a branch is now possible** for some
long-stay residents, through the banks' own apps. **Toss Bank** in
particular has supported opening using the NFC chip in a biometric
passport, which is unusual because it does not lean on a Korean
phone number.

The general shape: **internet-only banks — Kakao Bank, Toss Bank,
K Bank — were historically hard or impossible for foreign
residents, and that is loosening.** F-4, F-5 and F-6 holders have
had the easiest time of it. This is an area where the answer has
genuinely changed, so it is worth asking rather than assuming.

### The name-match trap

One failure mode is worth its own warning, because it defeats
otherwise-correct people at the last step.

**Your name on the ARC must match your name with the phone carrier
exactly** — spacing, middle names, the lot. Non-face-to-face
identity verification compares the two strings, and "JOHN A SMITH"
against "JOHN SMITH" is a failure, not a near-miss.

If remote opening keeps rejecting you and you cannot see why, this
is the first thing to check.

## Online banking, and the certificate

Once the account exists you will want the app, and there you meet
Korea's identity layer: a **digital certificate** (공동인증서 /
금융인증서) that signs transactions.

Two practical notes. **Set it up at the branch while you are
there** — the staff do this all day and it is much faster than
fighting the app alone. And **a Korean phone number in your own
name** is doing a lot of work in this system; if the number is not
yours, expect friction at every verification step.

## Sending money home

Most people open the account partly for this. Two things worth
knowing before you plan around it:

- **Remittance abroad is documented.** Depending on the amount and
  the reason, the bank will want to see what the money is and where
  it came from — an employment contract, a tax record
- **Banks are not the cheapest route** for small amounts. Dedicated
  remittance services usually beat them on rate and fee, though the
  bank is the one that will still be there when the sum is large

## Small things that save a second trip

- **Bring originals and photocopies of everything.** Branches
  routinely want both, and a photocopier hunt mid-appointment is
  avoidable
- **Go in the morning.** Korean branches close early by
  international standards and the queue builds through the day
- **Set up the app and the certificate while you are at the
  counter** — the staff do this all day and it is far faster than
  fighting it alone afterwards
- **Ask outright what your limits are** and what would lift them.
  Written down, in the branch, before you leave

## The Korean part

The account itself is not the hard part. What is hard is that this
is a counter errand conducted in Korean, at a branch, during
weekday banking hours, with documents whose names you have not
learned yet — and where "come back with a different paper" costs
you another morning off work.

## Get it sorted

The one-line version: **you need the ARC, a Korean phone number and
your passport; the account opens with a limit of roughly ₩1,000,000
a day online, which is normal rather than a fault; you lift it by
taking proof of employment or address to the branch — and since
2025 a mobile ARC and remote opening have become real options, so
check what your bank allows before you take a morning off.**
