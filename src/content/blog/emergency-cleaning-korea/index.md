The message arrives at eleven in the morning. The landlord is coming at two.
Or the boiler technician. Or your partner's parents, who are already on the
train. And your flat looks like your flat.

**This page is for the next two hours.** Not for getting your home clean — for
getting the mess level down far enough that nobody comments, in the time you
actually have, without buying anything.

There is a version of this advice on every English-language cleaning site, and
**the first step on all of them does not work in Korea.** So start there.

## Why does the usual advice break here?

Every emergency-cleaning checklist written in English begins the same way:
gather all the rubbish and take the bags out.

In Korea that instruction can be **illegal at eleven in the morning.**

Three things make it so, and they are worth knowing cold:

- **Bags are district-specific.** General waste goes only in your district's
  official 종량제 봉투, bought at a convenience store counter. A Mapo bag does
  not work in Gangnam, and a black bin liner on the pavement is the thing that
  gets you fined.
- **Districts set put-out hours**, usually an evening window on the collection
  night — not whenever you are panicking.
- **Food waste is separate and mandatory**, and so is recycling by material.

Fines commonly start around ₩100,000, residential dumping spots are on camera,
and building offices do check. [The whole system, once and for
all](/blog/how-to-throw-away-trash-in-korea/) is the long version.

**So the rule for today is: bag it, do not bin it.** Everything gets sorted
into tied bags and staged out of sight. The bags leave the building tonight,
at the hour your building actually collects. That single change makes the rest
of this plan work.

## Before you touch anything: who is coming?

Twenty seconds of thinking here saves you forty minutes of cleaning the wrong
room. **Different visitors look at completely different things.**

| Who is coming | What they actually see |
|---|---|
| **Boiler, aircon or appliance technician (기사님)** | The route from the front door to the machine, and the space around it. Nothing else. They have seen worse today |
| **Landlord or estate agent** | Walls, ceilings, window frames, the balcony. They are checking the **building**, not your tidiness |
| **Agent bringing viewers** | Light and floor space. Clutter reads as "small flat" |
| **Friends, a 집들이** | The 현관, wherever they sit, and **the bathroom** |
| **Your partner's parents** | The kitchen sink and the bathroom. In that order |

Two of those are liberating. **A technician needs a path and a metre of
clearance, and that is twenty minutes of work, not two hours.** A landlord
checking for damp is not scoring your laundry pile — if anything, you want
them looking at the ceiling corner you have been meaning to report.

Pick the row that applies and clean for that person. Everything below assumes
the hardest case: people who are going to sit down.

## The two-hour plan

Set a timer on your phone for each block. The timer is not a gimmick — the
single biggest failure in a panic clean is spending fifty minutes on one
drawer.

### 0:00–0:15 — supplies, and somewhere to put things

**Use what you own.** Do not spend your first half hour shopping, and do not
order anything: even Korea's overnight delivery arrives tomorrow, which is
after the problem.

If you genuinely have nothing, **다이소 is the five-minute answer** and three
items cover almost everything: 물티슈 (wet wipes), 청소포 (disposable floor
sheets for a flat mop), and a pack of rubber gloves. Buy your 종량제 봉투 at
any convenience store counter on the way back — ask for them by name and by
size, and say your district.

Then decide **where things go while you work.** You need a staging area that
is not the middle of a room: the 베란다, the 다용도실, or one bedroom whose
door stays shut.

**One warning about that**, because this is how Korean flats end up with a
balcony nobody uses: a staging area is temporary. Write yourself a note with
tomorrow's date on it. If you are honest that it will not get undone, you are
not emergency cleaning, you are [putting off a sort-out that needs its own
day](/blog/deep-clean-korea-sorting-first/).

### 0:15–0:40 — bag it, don't bin it

One bag in each hand, one room at a time, and do not stop to make decisions.

- **General waste** into the 종량제 봉투.
- **Recycling** into a separate bag — paper, plastic, PET, glass, cans, and
  film. It does not have to be perfectly sorted right now; it has to be out of
  the room.
- **Food waste** into its own bag or your building's RFID bin. If you are not
  putting it out tonight, **put the sealed food-waste bag in the freezer.**
  This is not a trick — it is ordinary practice in Korean flats in summer, and
  it is the single most effective thing on this page for smell.

Tie every bag. Stage them in the spot you chose. **Do not carry them outside
unless it is your collection window** — a stack of bags by a building entrance
at midday is exactly what the fine is for.

If what you are facing is not a few bags but a room of them, that is a
different job and a different page: [trash removal in
Seoul](/blog/trash-removal-service-seoul/).

### 0:40–1:05 — the floor, and the 현관

**This is where Korean flats differ most, and where the time pays back
hardest.**

Korean homes are floor-oriented. The heating is in the floor, people sit on
it, and visitors take their shoes off and stand in your entrance for thirty
seconds with nothing to do but look. **A clear floor reads as a clean home here
in a way that a clear worktop does not.**

So, in order:

1. **The 현관.** Everything but one pair of shoes per person goes into the
   shoe cupboard or a bag. This is the first thing anyone sees and it takes
   four minutes.
2. **Pick things up off the floor.** Not sort — pick up. Into the staging
   area, or into the right room, and keep moving.
3. **The 빨래건조대.** The drying rack in the middle of the living room is
   the most recognisable sight in Korean domestic life and it makes any room
   look like a laundry. Dry clothes go away; damp ones go to the balcony with
   the rack.
4. **Clear the flat surfaces you can see.** Table, counter, the top of the
   fridge. Into a box, into a drawer. **Do not start organising the box.**
5. **청소포 on a flat mop**, straight across every floor you can reach. One
   sheet per room, no bucket, no wringing. Four minutes a room.

### 1:05–1:30 — the bathroom, then the sink

If people are going to be in your home for more than an hour, **they will go
into your bathroom**, and it is the room that gets judged hardest.

Twelve minutes, in this order: wipe the basin and the taps, do the toilet
including the base and the seat hinges, wipe the mirror, swap the towel for a
clean one, put the bin out of sight or empty it. **Skip the shower screen and
the grout.** Mould in the silicone is not a two-hour problem — it is [a
different conversation about whether cleaning can fix it at
all](/blog/bathroom-deep-clean-seoul/).

Then the kitchen: **wash what fits in fifteen minutes and put the rest in the
dishwasher or out of sight.** An empty sink with a wiped rim reads as a clean
kitchen even when the cupboards are chaos. A half-washed pile does not.

### 1:30–1:45 — the thing that actually gives you away

You cannot smell your own home. Everybody who walks in can.

Three Korean-specific sources, in the order they are likely:

- **Food waste.** Dealt with above. It is almost always this one.
- **The drains.** A Korean bathroom floor drain, and any sink you rarely use,
  holds a small water seal that stops the smell from the pipe coming back up.
  **In a dry month, or a bathroom nobody showers in, that water evaporates and
  the drain starts breathing at you.** The fix is thirty seconds: pour a mug
  of water down every drain in the flat, including the ones you forgot you
  had. [The twenty-minute version of this
  job](/blog/clean-filters-drains-yourself-korea/) covers the trap properly
  when you have time.
- **Shoes, in the 현관.** Into the cupboard with the door shut.

Then open every window for ten minutes, even in winter — **especially** in
winter, when flats are sealed. Korean apartments ventilate fast.

**Only after that** should you light anything or spray anything. Scent on top
of smell is worse than smell, and it is obvious.

### 1:45–2:00 — walk out and walk back in

Put your shoes on, go out of the front door, stand on the landing for ten
seconds, and come back in as a visitor.

You will see three things you have been walking past all morning. Deal with
exactly those three, then stop.

Then: shower, change into clothes you did not clean in, and eat something.
**Answering the door out of breath in a damp t-shirt undoes a surprising
amount of the work.**

## What should you not do with two hours?

Every one of these feels productive and costs you the deadline:

- **Do not deep clean one thing.** A gleaming oven in a messy flat impresses
  nobody and eats forty minutes.
- **Do not start sorting.** The moment you are making keep-or-bin decisions,
  you have stopped emergency cleaning. That is [a job with its own
  day](/blog/home-organising-korea-jeongni-sunap/).
- **Do not open a cupboard you cannot close again.** Today is not the day.
- **Do not move furniture.** Nothing behind it is being inspected.
- **Do not clean what your visitor is not there to see.** Re-read the table.

And one that catches foreign residents specifically: **do not put bags out
early just to get them out of sight.** It is the one action on this list that
can cost you money.

## What if the visit is an inspection?

Different problem. If this is a move-out walkthrough or a deposit
conversation, tidiness is not what is being argued about — **condition is.**

The thing that settles those arguments is not how clean the flat is this
afternoon. It is [the photographs you took on the day you moved
in](/blog/photograph-your-flat-move-in-day/), repeated from the same angles on
the way out. If you have two hours and a deposit at stake, **spend twenty
minutes of it taking photographs** and the rest on the plan above.

## If you have two days instead of two hours

Then do not do any of this.

**We cannot send a cleaner this afternoon, and neither can anybody good.**
Cleaning crews in Seoul are booked days out, the ones with same-day gaps
generally have them for a reason, and a rushed crew in a flat they have not
been quoted on is how people end up paying for a job they are unhappy with.

What we ask for is **two to three days, and ideally a week.** With that, the

**Two hours is triage. Two days is a solution.** The useful thing to take from
