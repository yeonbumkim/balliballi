Korean healthcare is genuinely excellent, genuinely cheap with
insurance, and genuinely fast — you can often walk in and be seen
within the hour. The part nobody explains is the *first* decision:
**which clinic do you even walk into?** There's no family doctor
gatekeeping the system here. You pick the specialty yourself, and
picking wrong costs you a wasted afternoon and a second copay.

Here's how the system is laid out, and how to land in the right
room on the first try.

> **Note:** this article is general information about how Korea's
> clinic system is organized. It is **not medical advice**, and it
> is **not an advertisement or promotion for any medical
> institution**. For anything about your health, ask a licensed
> doctor.

## First: clinic, hospital, or ER?

Three tiers, and starting at the right one saves money and time:

- **의원 (uiwon) — local clinic.** One or two doctors, a single
  specialty, usually on the 2nd–5th floor of a commercial building
  with a sign by the elevator. **This is where you start** for
  anything routine.
- **병원 / 종합병원 — hospital / general hospital.** Multiple
  departments, imaging, admissions. For serious cases, or when a
  clinic refers you. Going here first for a sore throat means
  longer waits and higher fees.
- **응급실 — emergency room.** Genuine emergencies only —
  **call 119** for an ambulance
  ([and here is what the other numbers do](/blog/korea-emergency-numbers-112-119/),
  including the fact that you can text them). ER visits are expensive by Korean
  standards and slow for non-urgent problems.

The big-hospital fees are also structured to push you toward
clinics first: without a referral, the insurance share is worse.

## The department decoder

Korean clinic signs are specialty names, so this table is the
whole trick. Read the Korean, match your symptom:

| Your problem | Department | Korean |
| ------------ | ---------- | ------ |
| Cold, flu, fever, stomach upset, general illness | Internal medicine | **내과** |
| Sore throat, cough, blocked nose, sinus, ear, dizziness | ENT | **이비인후과** |
| Anything you can't classify; check-ups; vaccines | Family medicine (closest to a GP) | **가정의학과** |
| Broken bones, sprains, back/knee/shoulder pain, sports injuries | Orthopedics | **정형외과** |
| Spine, disc, nerve pain, headaches after injury | Neurosurgery | **신경외과** |
| Rashes, acne, eczema, hair loss, moles | Dermatology | **피부과** |
| Eyes, vision, styes | Ophthalmology | **안과** |
| Teeth | Dentistry | **치과** |
| Pregnancy, periods, contraception, women's health | OB-GYN | **산부인과** |
| Children (usually under 15) | Pediatrics | **소아청소년과** |
| Urinary, kidney stones, men's health | Urology | **비뇨의학과** |
| Anxiety, depression, sleep, ADHD | Psychiatry | **정신건강의학과** |
| Allergies, asthma | Internal medicine (allergy) | **알레르기내과** |
| Acupuncture, herbal medicine, cupping, chronic aches | Korean medicine | **한의원** |

Two things surprise Western patients most. First, **Koreans go to
ENT for a common cold** — if your symptoms are above the neck,
이비인후과 is the local instinct, and they'll treat it faster and
more thoroughly than you expect. Second, **you can walk straight
into a specialist**: no referral, no gatekeeper, no two-week wait
for a GP appointment. If you genuinely don't know where to go,
**가정의학과 (family medicine)** is the closest thing to a general
practitioner and will redirect you if needed.

## The parallel system: 한의원 (Korean medicine)

One category sits outside the Western-medicine map entirely.
**한의원 (haniwon)** are clinics of **Korean traditional medicine**
— acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, herbal prescriptions and
*chuna* manual therapy — staffed by separately licensed doctors of
Korean medicine, with their own six-year training track. They are
everywhere, they are regulated, and a lot of Koreans use both
systems depending on the problem.

What locals typically bring there: **chronic back and neck pain,
sports and posture problems, digestive complaints, fatigue,
recovery after an accident.** Acupuncture and chuna for
musculoskeletal issues are **partially covered by national
insurance**, which makes a session surprisingly affordable; herbal
prescriptions (한약) are generally not covered and are the
expensive part.

It's also become a wellness draw for visitors — a fair share of
tourists now come specifically for acupuncture or herbal
consultations. If that's you, two practical notes: **English
support varies enormously** (some clinics in Gangnam and Itaewon
are set up for foreign patients, most neighborhood ones are not),
and **an herbal prescription is a real medical prescription** —
tell the doctor about any medication you're taking and any dietary
restrictions, including if you're vegetarian, since some formulas
use animal-derived ingredients.

## What a visit costs

With **National Health Insurance** — which is mandatory for
residents staying six months or more — a routine clinic visit
plus a prescription typically lands in the **₩5,000–15,000**
range out of pocket, as of 2026. Pharmacies are almost always in
the same building or next door, and prescriptions are filled in
minutes.

Without insurance (tourists, or before your enrollment kicks in),
you pay the full price — still modest by American standards, but
several times higher.

Clinics and pharmacies take cards, and — since the question comes
up — **you don't tip your doctor, your nurse or your pharmacist.**
Nobody tips anybody here, in healthcare or anywhere else
([the full story](/blog/no-tipping-in-korea/)).

## Finding a clinic where English works

Here's the honest picture: **most neighborhood clinics run in
Korean.** Doctors often read English well and speak it unevenly;
reception and nurses usually don't. Your realistic options:

- **Large general hospitals** often have an **international
  healthcare center** with English-speaking coordinators — the
  most reliable English, at hospital prices.
- **Expat-dense neighborhoods** (Itaewon, Hannam, Gangnam,
  university districts) have clinics used to foreign patients.
- **1330**, Korea's 24/7 travel helpline, answers in English and
  can help you find and call a clinic.
- **119** for emergencies — English support is available on the
  line.
- **Ask before you go.** A two-minute phone call — "do you have
  anyone who speaks English?" — saves an entire wasted trip. That
  call is the part that stops most people.

## Before you go: the practical checklist

- **Bring your ARC** (and insurance card if you have one) — the
  registration desk needs your foreigner registration number.
- **Write your symptoms down** before you arrive, in simple terms,
  and translate them. "Sore throat, 3 days, fever 38, no cough"
  beats improvised charades.
- **Know your allergies and current medications** in writing.
- **Expect efficiency, not chat.** Korean consultations are short
  and to the point. That's not rudeness; ask your questions
  directly and you'll get direct answers.
- **Take the prescription to the pharmacy** — usually downstairs.
  Medication is dispensed per dose in strips; take exactly what
  the label says.
